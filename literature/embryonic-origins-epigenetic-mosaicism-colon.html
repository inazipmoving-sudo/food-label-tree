Embryonic origins of epigenetic mosaicism in the human colorectal epithelium

Young Seok Ju, Hyein Won, Yunah Lee … Min Jung Kim

Research Square (preprint) · 29 Oct 2026 · Preprint

← Back to the Longevity course

Preprint, not yet peer reviewed. These results have not been checked by independent reviewers and may change.

Read the original paper →doi:10.21203/rs.3.rs-11351803/v1

Open access. The full paper is free to read.

Most of the DNA methylation differences between colon cells in a 60-year-old seem to be set in the embryo, not picked up with age, which changes how we read epigenetic drift.

Why it matters

DNA methylation is a chemical tag on DNA, mostly at sites called CpGs, that helps decide which genes are switched on. As we age, the methylation patterns of cells of the same type drift apart, so a tissue becomes a patchwork, or mosaic, of slightly different epigenetic states. This drift is the basis of epigenetic clocks and is often described as one of the hallmarks of aging. But it has been hard to tell how much of that mosaic really builds up over a lifetime and how much was there from the start. If many differences are laid down before birth, then not every methylation difference seen in an older person is a sign of aging. This preprint tries to separate the two.

What they did

According to the abstract, the team grew clones, each started from a single cell, from the colorectal lining (epithelium) of 16 donors, 406 clones in all. For each clone they read both the whole genome, to find somatic mutations (DNA changes acquired after conception), and the whole methylome, the methylation state across the genome. Because the clones share an ancestry that can be traced through their mutations, the researchers could work out when along the family tree of cells each methylation difference arose: early in the embryo, or later in adult life. They then looked at where the early differences sit in the genome, whether they relate to gene activity in adult colon tissue, and whether the same idea applies to X-chromosome inactivation in female cells.

What they found

By around 60 years of age, a single colon cell lineage differed from the consensus methylation state at about 1.2 million CpG sites. The striking result is that about 60% of these differences were established during early embryogenesis and then copied faithfully through life, rather than being epimutations that accumulate with age. These early differences tended to cluster and recur in the same regions, which the authors read as cell lineages committing early to one of a few possible methylation states, not random errors. They mapped 14,402 such regions, which they call embryonic epiallelic commitment (EEC) regions. These were enriched at CpG islands and gene promoters, especially homeobox genes and zinc-finger/KRAB-domain genes, both linked to early development. EECs were often associated with differences in gene expression in the adult colon. They also explained more than 70% of the loss of methylation at LINE-1 promoters commonly seen in adult colon tissue, and they helped reconstruct early developmental family trees of cells more accurately than mutations alone. A similar pattern held for the methylation behind X-chromosome inactivation and escape.

Limits

This is a preprint on Research Square and has not yet been peer reviewed, so the results may change. This synopsis is based on the abstract only. The study covers one tissue, the colorectal epithelium, from 16 donors, and the abstract does not say how well the findings carry over to blood or other tissues where epigenetic clocks are usually measured. The link between EECs and gene expression is an association. About 40% of differences are still not embryonic, so age-related epimutation remains part of the picture.

Key terms

DNA methylation
A small chemical tag added to DNA, mainly at CpG sites, that helps control whether genes are active.
CpG site
A spot in DNA where the base C is followed by G; the main place methylation tags are added.
Epigenetic mosaicism
When cells of the same type in one person carry different methylation patterns.
Epimutation
A change in an epigenetic mark, such as methylation, that is passed on when a cell divides.
Clone (single-cell-derived)
A group of cells grown from one starting cell, so all share that cell's DNA and marks.
LINE-1
A family of repeated 'jumping gene' sequences in the genome that are normally kept silent by methylation.
