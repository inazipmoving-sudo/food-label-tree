Investigating the efficacy and toxicity of partial in vivo reprogramming as a rejuvenation intervention

Franziska Meiners, Daniel Palmer, Roberto A Avelar, Israel Barrantes, Georg Fuellen

Aging (Albany NY) · 27 Oct 2026 · Research

← Back to the Longevity course

Read the original paper →doi:10.18632/aging.206431

Open access. The full paper is free to read (free copy).

A re-analysis finds that partial reprogramming looked rejuvenating in liver and kidney but flagged possible genomic and proliferative stress in the spleen.

Why it matters

Partial reprogramming means briefly switching on the Yamanaka factors, the genes that can turn adult cells back into stem cells, in short repeated cycles. The hope is to wind back the epigenetic marks that change with age without erasing a cell's identity. It is one of the most discussed rejuvenation ideas, but forcing cells toward a younger, more dividing state could also raise the risk of uncontrolled growth and stress on the genome. Responses may also differ between organs. This paper looks at both sides, possible benefits and possible harms, across several tissues.

What they did

The authors did not run a new experiment. According to the abstract, they reanalysed existing RNA sequencing data, a readout of which genes are active, from the liver, kidney and spleen of aged mice that had received seven months of cyclic partial reprogramming. They used the Adverse Outcome Pathway (AOP) framework, a toxicology approach that links molecular changes to possible harmful or beneficial outcomes. They also ran gene set enrichment analysis to see which biological processes shifted, and used deconvolution, a computational method that estimates which cell types make up a tissue sample from bulk data.

What they found

In the liver and kidney, reprogramming dampened immune and inflammatory processes that normally rise with age and switched back on metabolic and breakdown pathways that normally fall with age. In the liver, the AOP analysis pointed to changes opposite to fatty liver disease (steatosis), including activation of the nuclear receptors Ppara and Pparg and restoration of fatty-acid breakdown. In the kidney, the AOP framework found no enriched key events when comparing reprogrammed and old animals. The spleen looked different: programs for the cell cycle, DNA replication and the DNA damage response were prominent, suggesting more cell division. The estimated share of megakaryocyte-erythroid progenitor cells, which make platelets and red cells, went up and natural killer cells went down, and the AOP analysis flagged a possible risk of sustained genomic and proliferative stress.

Limits

This synopsis is based on the abstract only, although the paper is open access. The results come from re-analysing gene activity data, which shows patterns of gene use, not actual damage, tumours or organ function. The flagged genomic stress in the spleen is a predicted risk from the framework, not a measured outcome. Cell type proportions were estimated computationally rather than counted. The original study's size is not given in the abstract, and the authors themselves call for larger, controlled studies to clarify benefits and safety.

Key terms

Partial reprogramming
Brief, repeated activation of Yamanaka factors to make cells more youthful without turning them into stem cells.
Yamanaka factors
Four genes (Oct4, Sox2, Klf4, c-Myc) that can reprogram adult cells into stem cells.
RNA sequencing
A method that reads all the RNA in a sample to show which genes are switched on.
Adverse Outcome Pathway (AOP)
A toxicology framework linking molecular events step by step to harmful health effects.
DNA damage response
The set of cell signals that detect DNA damage, pause division and start repair.
Deconvolution
A computational way to estimate the mix of cell types in a tissue from bulk gene data.
