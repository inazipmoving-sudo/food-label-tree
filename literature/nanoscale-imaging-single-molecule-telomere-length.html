A high-throughput nanoscale imaging platform for direct visualization and rapid measurement of telomeres at single-molecule resolution

Collen Jackson-Cook, Lilia Gheghiani, Andrey Mikheykin, Dylan Van Laeken, Daniel Guest, Kellie Archer, Harold Riethman, Jason C Reed

Research Square (preprint) · 28 Oct 2026 · Preprint

← Back to the Longevity course

Preprint, not yet peer reviewed. These results have not been checked by independent reviewers and may change.

Read the original paper →doi:10.21203/rs.3.rs-11283393/v1

Open access. The full paper is free to read (free copy).

A simpler way to see every telomere's length one molecule at a time could catch the critically short ones that drive senescence.

Why it matters

Telomeres are protective caps of repeated DNA at the ends of chromosomes, and they shorten as cells divide. Telomere length is used as a marker of aging, cancer and inherited telomere diseases. Some of the most important health links involve the extremes: a few critically short telomeres can trigger chromosome instability, cellular stress and senescence, even when the average length looks normal. Most standard tests report only an average for a whole sample, which hides those few short ends, and they can be thrown off by the repetitive sequence or vary between labs. Newer high-resolution methods exist but often need a lot of DNA, special preparation or complex equipment. This preprint presents a method meant to make single-telomere measurement practical.

What they did

According to the abstract, the team built an automated imaging workflow that works with standard genomic DNA preparations. It uses two CRISPR-Cas9 cuts to mark the boundaries of each telomere, then pulls telomere pieces out with magnetic beads without copying (amplifying) them, which helps keep the natural mix of lengths. The pieces are then imaged at nanoscale resolution and measured one at a time. They tested reproducibility across repeat runs and different operators, and validated the method in models with known telomere lengths, in cells aged by repeated division, and in clinical samples. They also added subtelomeric barcoding, tags that show which chromosome arm each telomere comes from.

What they found

The method needed only 500 ng of input DNA and 2 to 4 hours of hands-on work. It resolved intact telomeres from about 50 base pairs to more than 50 kilobases in the same sample, capturing the full spread of lengths at once, including very short ones. Measurements were highly reproducible across technical replicates and different operators, with an intraclass correlation (a measure of agreement) of up to 0.90. The validation experiments and the barcoding support its use for chromosome-by-chromosome telomere profiling. The authors present it as a scalable platform for biomarker development and possible clinical use in aging, cancer and telomere biology.

Limits

This is a preprint on Research Square and has not been peer reviewed. This synopsis is based on the abstract only, although a full-text PDF is openly available. It is a methods paper: it shows the test can measure telomeres, not that the measurements predict disease or aging outcomes in people. The abstract does not say how many clinical samples were tested or how the method compares head to head with existing assays. Whether it can be adopted widely depends on access to its imaging equipment, which the abstract describes only briefly.

Key terms

Telomere
A stretch of repeated DNA at each chromosome end that protects it and shortens with cell division.
Critically short telomere
A telomere short enough to be read as DNA damage, which can stop the cell dividing.
CRISPR-Cas9
A tool that cuts DNA at a chosen sequence; here it marks where telomeres begin.
Amplification bias
Distortion that happens when DNA is copied before measurement, especially with repetitive sequences.
Subtelomere
The DNA just next to a telomere, which differs by chromosome and can show which chromosome arm it is.
Intraclass correlation (ICC)
A score from 0 to 1 for how well repeated measurements agree.
