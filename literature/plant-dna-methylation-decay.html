Aging drives a program of DNA methylation decay in plant organs

Dawei Dai, Ken Chen, Jingwen Tao, Ben P Williams

Science · 29 Jan 2026 · Research

Read the original paper →doi:10.1126/science.adu2392

Open access. The full paper is free to read (free copy).

Aging plants lose DNA methylation in a programmed way, but stopping that loss did not stop physical aging, so the marks may track rather than cause aging.

Why it matters

Changes in DNA methylation are a hallmark of aging in mammals and the basis of epigenetic clocks. A key open question is whether these changes cause aging or simply record it. This study tests that question in a plant, where genetics are easy to manipulate. Its answer, that methylation loss and physical aging can be separated, is an important caution for anyone treating clocks as the cause of aging rather than a readout of it. Plants are useful here because their DNA-methylation machinery can be knocked out in ways that would be lethal in mammals, letting researchers ask what happens to aging when the epigenetic changes are removed.

What they did

The researchers studied the short-lived model plant Arabidopsis thaliana. They tracked DNA methylation and the activity of transposable elements in organs as they aged, lengthened or shortened the plants' lifespan to see whether epigenetic aging sped up or slowed down, examined the shoot apical meristem (the growing tip), and identified and disabled a gene-repression programme that switches off methylation maintenance during aging.

What they found

Aging Arabidopsis organs lost epigenetic integrity: DNA methylation decayed and transposable elements became active. The rate of this epigenetic aging could be changed by lengthening or shortening the plant's lifespan, while the shoot tips were protected from it. A programme of transcriptional repression turned down the systems that maintain DNA methylation during aging. Mutant plants lacking this programme showed no epigenetic decay at all, yet their physical aging was unaffected.

Limits

Plants differ from animals in many ways, including how they grow and how their methylation systems work, so the results do not transfer directly to humans. The study uses one model species. The abstract shows that methylation decay can be uncoupled from physical aging in this plant; it does not settle the question for mammals. It also does not mean epigenetic clocks are useless in people: a marker can predict outcomes well even if it is not a cause, as the human clock studies in this list show.

Key terms

Arabidopsis thaliana
A small, fast-growing weed used as the standard model plant in genetics.
Transposable element
A stretch of DNA that can copy or move itself around the genome; normally kept silent by methylation.
Shoot apical meristem
The growing tip of a plant, containing stem cells that make new organs.
Epigenetic integrity
Keeping the normal pattern of DNA methylation and other marks intact.
Transcriptional repression
Switching genes off at the stage where they are read into RNA.
DNA methylation maintenance
The system that copies methylation marks onto new DNA so the pattern is kept.
