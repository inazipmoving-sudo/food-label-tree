Epigenetic clocks and nonlinear aging in Odocoileus deer

Luca Montana, Cheyenne Stewart, L. Embere Hall, Aaron B. A. Shafer

bioRxiv (preprint) · 27 Oct 2026 · Preprint

← Back to the Longevity course

Preprint, not yet peer reviewed. These results have not been checked by independent reviewers and may change.

Read the original paper →doi:10.64898/2026.10.23.761432

Open access. The full paper is free to read.

A new DNA-methylation clock for wild deer shows methylation changes with age are not a straight line, and short-term changes are hard to read.

Why it matters

Epigenetic clocks read the pattern of DNA methylation, small chemical tags on DNA, at many sites and turn it into an estimate of age. They are one of the main tools for studying how the genome's regulation drifts with age, in people and in other animals. For wildlife managers, knowing the ages of animals in a population matters for understanding how it will grow or shrink, but most wild animals cannot be aged easily. Deer and their relatives are among the most heavily managed wild animals, yet clocks existed for only two species in the deer family. Studying a wild species also tests whether patterns of epigenetic ageing seen in lab animals and people hold more broadly.

What they did

The researchers built a new epigenetic clock for mule deer (Odocoileus hemionus) and a multi-species clock meant to work across the Odocoileus genus, which also includes white-tailed deer. They used both clocks to look at how age-associated DNA methylation changes across the mule deer lifespan. They compared animals of different ages at one time (a cross-sectional view) and also followed changes in epigenetic age within individual animals over time (a longitudinal view). They also looked at single methylation sites, called CpGs, to find those most strongly tied to age.

What they found

The mule deer clock was about as accurate as recently built mammal clocks, with a correlation of about 0.9 with true age and an average error of about 5% of the species' lifespan. The multi-species clock was less accurate, with a correlation of 0.72 and an average error of 0.782 years, but the authors note it could be useful where Odocoileus species live together and interbreed. The cross-sectional estimates supported nonlinear methylation change across the lifespan, meaning it does not change at a steady rate. The longitudinal data suggested that clocks built to estimate age across a population may not be sensitive enough to measure short-term changes within one animal. The team also identified strongly age-linked sites that could be used for cheaper, targeted tests.

Limits

This is a preprint and has not been peer reviewed. The synopsis is based on the abstract only. The abstract does not give the number of animals or tissue type. The main aim is estimating age in wildlife, so the study speaks more to how epigenetic clocks behave than to the causes of ageing. The nonlinear pattern is described as 'supported' by cross-sectional data, which can be affected by which animals survive to old age.

Key terms

Epigenetic clock
A formula that estimates age from DNA methylation levels at a chosen set of sites in the genome.
DNA methylation
Small chemical tags (methyl groups) attached to DNA, mostly at CpG sites, that help control which genes are active.
CpG site
A place in DNA where the letter C is followed by G; the usual spot for methylation.
Cross-sectional vs longitudinal
Comparing different individuals at one time, versus following the same individuals over time.
MAE
Mean absolute error: the average size of the gap between estimated and true age.
Odocoileus
The genus of New World deer that includes mule deer and white-tailed deer.
