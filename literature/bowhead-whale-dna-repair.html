Evidence for improved DNA repair in the long-lived bowhead whale

Denis Firsanov, Max Zacher, Xiao Tian … Vera Gorbunova

Nature · 29 Oct 2025 · Research

← Back to the Longevity course

Read the original paper →doi:10.1038/s41586-025-09694-5

Open access. The full paper is free to read.

A 200-year-old mammal seems to stay healthy by repairing DNA breaks more accurately, not by killing damaged cells.

Why it matters

Bowhead whales can live for more than 200 years and weigh over 80,000 kg, yet they are not especially prone to cancer. With that many cells dividing for that long, you would expect far more cancer, a puzzle known as Peto's paradox. If we knew how the whale protects its genome, we would learn something about why DNA damage builds up in people as we age, and about which repair systems are worth strengthening. This paper is one of the clearest tests so far of the idea that better DNA repair, rather than extra tumour-suppressor genes, can buy a long life.

What they did

The team grew skin-type cells (fibroblasts) from bowhead whales and compared them with cells from humans and other mammals. First they counted how many cancer-causing genetic "hits" it took to turn the cells malignant. Then they measured how well the cells fixed double-strand breaks, the most dangerous kind of DNA damage, and how often new mutations appeared. When one protein, CIRBP, stood out as unusually abundant in the whale, they put the whale version into human cells and into fruit flies to see what it changed.

What they found

Surprisingly, whale cells needed fewer cancer-causing hits than human cells to become malignant, so the whale is not protected by a harder-to-break cancer barrier. Instead, whale cells repaired double-strand breaks more often and more faithfully, and they built up mutations more slowly than cells of other mammals. CIRBP, a cold-inducible RNA-binding protein, was highly expressed in whale cells and tissues. The whale form of CIRBP boosted both main break-repair routes (non-homologous end joining and homologous recombination) in human cells, cut down micronuclei (small fragments of broken chromosome), protected broken DNA ends, and stimulated end joining in a test tube. Extra CIRBP extended lifespan in fruit flies and made them more resistant to radiation. The authors conclude that the whale keeps its genome intact by repairing damage well rather than by getting rid of damaged cells.

Limits

Most of the work was done on cultured cells and in fruit flies, not in living whales or people, and the fly lifespan result comes from overexpressing the protein. The abstract does not show that raising CIRBP would make humans live longer or lower cancer risk. Comparing species also leaves room for many other differences between whales and humans to matter.

Key terms

Double-strand break
Both strands of the DNA helix cut at the same place; the most dangerous kind of DNA damage.
Non-homologous end joining (NHEJ)
A quick repair route that glues broken DNA ends back together, sometimes losing a few letters.
Homologous recombination (HR)
A more accurate repair route that copies the missing sequence from the matching chromosome.
CIRBP
Cold-inducible RNA-binding protein, made in larger amounts when cells are cooled.
Peto's paradox
Large, long-lived animals get no more cancer than small ones, despite having far more cells.
