Predicting biological age and clinical biomarkers from DNA methylation profiles of cheek mucosa

Tatsuma Shoji, Yui Tomo, Ryo Nakaki

Epigenetics Communications · 29 Oct 2026 · Research

← Back to the Longevity course

Read the original paper →doi:10.1186/s43682-026-00055-5

Open access. The full paper is free to read.

A cheek sample, instead of blood, predicted chronological and phenotypic age about as well as blood-based epigenetic clocks in 186 Japanese adults.

Why it matters

Epigenetic clocks estimate how old the body seems from DNA methylation, chemical tags on DNA that shift in predictable ways with age. Most clocks were built from blood, which means a needle and a lab draw. A cheek swab is far easier to collect, but cheek cells are a different tissue and their methylation patterns differ from blood. This study asks whether cheek tissue can stand in for blood, not only for estimating age but also for predicting a range of everyday clinical lab results.

What they did

According to the abstract, the researchers measured DNA methylation in both cheek mucosa (the inner lining of the cheek) and whole blood from 186 Japanese adults, using a newer Illumina chip called the Infinium Methylation Screening Array (MSA). They built models to predict chronological age, phenotypic age (a measure of biological age based on clinical blood tests) and individual lab biomarkers. Besides a standard method, elastic net regression, they used a two-stage residual learning method that starts from existing blood-based epigenetic clocks and then learns a correction. They also ran analyses separately for men and women and compared which CpG sites the models picked across sexes and tissues.

What they found

Cheek-derived methylation data predicted chronological age with a correlation of R = 0.965 and phenotypic age with R = 0.964 using the two-stage method. The two-stage approach helped more for phenotypic age than for chronological age. Several clinical lab markers could be predicted from cheek methylation, especially when men and women were modelled separately, including inflammatory, metabolic, thyroid-related and sex hormone-related markers. Most markers that blood methylation could predict could also be predicted from cheek samples. However, the specific CpG sites the models relied on overlapped very little between sexes and between tissues, even when the same trait was predictable. The authors conclude that cheek mucosa is a less invasive alternative for methylation-based assessment of biological aging and overall physiological state.

Limits

This synopsis is based on the abstract only, although the paper is open access. The sample is modest, 186 adults, all Japanese, so results may not transfer to other populations. The abstract does not say whether the models were tested in a separate, independent group of people. Phenotypic age is itself an estimate built from blood tests, and predicting it well does not show the cheek clock predicts disease or death. The low overlap in selected CpG sites means the models may be fragile or specific to this chip and setting.

Key terms

Epigenetic clock
A formula that estimates age from DNA methylation levels at selected sites.
Cheek mucosa
The soft inner lining of the cheek, sampled with a simple swab or scrape.
Phenotypic age
A biological age estimate calculated from routine clinical blood tests and chronological age.
Elastic net
A statistical method that picks a small set of useful predictors from many candidates.
Residual learning
Starting from an existing model's prediction and training a second model to correct its errors.
CpG site
A spot in DNA where C is followed by G, where methylation tags are usually added.
