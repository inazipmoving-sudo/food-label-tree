Tubulin acetylation regulates nuclear shape fluctuations and DNA repair

Sam Wallis, Wai Yiu Tse, Lisa Donker … Susana A Godinho

Nature Cell Biology · 28 Oct 2026 · Research

Read the original paper →doi:10.1038/s41556-026-02096-3

Open access. The full paper is free to read.

Cells seem to fix broken DNA better by letting the nucleus wobble, and blocking that wobble pushes them into senescence.

Why it matters

A double-strand break, where both strands of the DNA helix are cut, is one of the most dangerous things that can happen to a genome. Unrepaired or badly repaired breaks are a major source of the mutations and chromosome damage that pile up as we age, and cells that cannot fix them often stop dividing for good and become senescent. Most research on repair has looked at the chemistry of chromatin, the DNA and its packaging proteins. This paper looks at something more physical: whether the forces of the cell's internal skeleton, pushing and pulling on the nucleus, help decide whether a break gets fixed properly. If they do, it links the mechanics of the cell to genome stability and to the senescent cells that accumulate in ageing tissue.

What they did

According to the abstract, the researchers studied cells after inducing DNA damage and followed what happened to the cytoskeleton, the network of protein filaments that shapes the cell. They focused on microtubules, hollow tubes built from tubulin, and on a chemical tag called acetylation that can be added to tubulin. They also tracked vimentin, a filament that normally forms a cage around the nucleus. They then blocked tubulin acetylation to see how nuclear shape, the movement of broken DNA and repair by homologous recombination changed, and in a separate step they removed the vimentin cage to see whether that reversed the effects. The abstract does not give the cell types, the exact methods or sample sizes.

What they found

DNA damage caused tubulin acetylation to build up step by step. That shifted the vimentin cage away from the nucleus, which made the nucleus softer and let microtubule forces act on the nuclear envelope. The result was brief shape fluctuations of the nucleus, more local movement of the broken DNA sites, and better repair. When the authors blocked tubulin acetylation, the fluctuations stopped, the breaks moved less and the homology search, the step where a broken chromosome finds a matching copy to copy from, was impaired. Non-homologous end joining, the faster and less exact repair route, was not affected. The persistent breaks gathered and clustered at the edge of the nucleus, the cells arrested their division cycle and became senescent, and they were more sensitive to DNA-damaging drugs. Removing the vimentin cage was enough to restore the nuclear fluctuations and repair efficiency. The authors propose that nuclear fluctuations are a mechanical way for cells to speed up error-free repair.

Limits

This synopsis is based on the abstract only, although the paper is open access. The abstract does not say which cells or organisms were used, so it is not clear how directly the results apply to ageing human tissue. The work describes a mechanism in cells; it does not test whether loss of this mechanism contributes to ageing in a living animal, and it reports no treatment or human outcome. The proposed role of nuclear fluctuations as a general principle is the authors' interpretation and will need confirmation in other systems.

Key terms

Double-strand break
Both strands of the DNA helix cut at the same place; the most dangerous kind of DNA damage.
Homologous recombination (HR)
An accurate repair route that copies the missing information from a matching DNA sequence, usually the sister chromosome.
Non-homologous end joining (NHEJ)
A quick repair route that glues broken ends back together, sometimes losing a few DNA letters.
Microtubules and tubulin acetylation
Microtubules are tube-shaped filaments made of tubulin; acetylation is a small chemical tag added to tubulin that changes how the tubes behave.
Vimentin
A protein that forms flexible filaments; here it builds a cage that stiffens and protects the nucleus.
Senescence
A state in which a cell permanently stops dividing but stays alive; senescent cells build up with age.
