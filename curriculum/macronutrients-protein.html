Module 1 · Protein

  1. 1.1

    What Protein Is

    Protein is the next macronutrient in this course. Like fat it gives energy, about four calories per gram, the same as carbohydrate. But energy isn't its main job. Protein is what your body is built from and what does most of its work: muscle, skin and hair, the enzymes that run every chemical reaction, the antibodies that fight infection, and the hemoglobin that carries oxygen in your blood.

    Title, then the Protein box at the top
  2. 1.2

    Amino Acids

    Every protein is made of smaller units called amino acids, the left branch. There are twenty of them. Nine are essential, meaning your body can't make them, so they have to come from food. The other eleven your body can make, although six of those, called conditionally essential, can run short when you're ill, injured or growing fast.

    Left branch: Amino acids, then Essential, Non-essential and Conditional
  3. 1.3

    Chains

    In the middle, amino acids join into chains. A short chain, up to about fifty, is called a peptide. A longer one folds into a working shape and becomes a protein. When you eat protein, you digest it back down into amino acids and use them to build your own.

    Middle branch: Chains, Peptides, Proteins, Digested
  4. 1.4

    Protein in Food

    On the right is protein in food. A complete protein has all nine essential amino acids in good amounts. An incomplete one is low in at least one, but two incomplete proteins can cover each other's gaps. Each box links to the figure that explains it.

    Right branch: In food, Complete, Incomplete, Combine them
  5. 1.5

    On the Label

    On a nutrition label, protein is just one line, in grams. In the US the percent Daily Value for protein is often left off; it's only required when the package makes a protein claim or the food is for young children. And the label won't tell you which amino acids are in there; Figure 7 shows how to tell from the food.

    The three boxes under the tree
  1. 2.1

    Four Kinds of Atom

    Let's build an amino acid from scratch. It uses only a few kinds of atom, and each always makes the same number of bonds. Carbon makes four, like this methane. Nitrogen makes three, like ammonia. Oxygen makes two, like water. Hydrogen makes just one. Sulfur, which appears in two amino acids, makes two, like hydrogen sulfide.

    The four small molecules, left to right
  2. 2.2

    Nitrogen Is New

    Fats and carbohydrates are made only of carbon, hydrogen and oxygen. Protein adds nitrogen. That's the atom that makes protein special, and later we'll see that your body has to do something with it when protein is burned for energy.

    Nitrogen (blue) in ammonia
  3. 2.3

    Glycine

    Here's the simplest amino acid, glycine. On the left is an amine group: a nitrogen with two hydrogens. That's the amino part of the name. In the middle is a carbon called the alpha carbon. On the right is a carboxyl group: a carbon with a double-bonded oxygen and an OH. That's the acid part, because the OH can give up its hydrogen.

    Markers 1, 2, 3 on glycine
  4. 2.4

    The Side Chain

    Now alanine. It has exactly the same amine group, alpha carbon and carboxyl group. The only difference is what hangs off the alpha carbon. In glycine it's just a hydrogen; in alanine it's a CH3, a methyl group. That hanging part is called the side chain, or R group, and it's the only thing that differs between all twenty amino acids.

    Marker 4: the side chain
  5. 2.5

    Charged in the Body

    One small detail: drawn here, the amine and carboxyl groups are neutral. In your body's fluids the carboxyl usually gives up its hydrogen and the amine picks one up, so each end carries a charge. The atoms are the same, just one hydrogen moved.

    Note
  1. 3.1

    Head to Tail

    Amino acids join head to tail. The carboxyl group of one lines up with the amine group of the next. The OH from the carboxyl and one hydrogen from the amine come off together, and that's a water molecule, H2O.

    Markers 1 and 2, then the water
  2. 3.2

    The Bond Itself

    What's left is a new bond between the carbon of the first amino acid and the nitrogen of the second. That carbon-nitrogen link, next to its C=O, is the peptide bond. It's strong and flat, which is part of why protein chains fold the way they do. Two amino acids joined like this make a dipeptide, here glycyl-alanine.

    Marker 3: the peptide bond
  3. 3.3

    Adding More

    Add a third, here serine, and you get a tripeptide with two peptide bonds, and another water released. The chain always has a free amine at one end, called the N-terminus, and a free carboxyl at the other, the C-terminus. New amino acids are added at the C-terminus end.

    N, markers 1 and 2, then C
  4. 3.4

    The Backbone

    Look along the chain: nitrogen, carbon, carbon, nitrogen, carbon, carbon. That repeating pattern is the backbone, and the side chains stick out from it, alternating sides. Most proteins are a few hundred amino acids long, some many thousands. Digestion is the reverse: water is added back to split each peptide bond.

    The N–C–C repeat along the zigzag
  1. 4.1

    One Backbone, Twenty Side Chains

    Here are all twenty, drawn at the same scale and the same way round: the amine and carboxyl along the bottom, the side chain pointing up. The bottoms all match. Only the tops differ, from glycine's single hydrogen to tryptophan's big double ring.

    Compare the bottoms, then the tops
  2. 4.2

    The Nine Essential

    These nine are essential: histidine, isoleucine, leucine, lysine, methionine, phenylalanine, threonine, tryptophan and valine. Your body has no way to make their side chains, so every one has to come from food. Three of them, leucine, isoleucine and valine, have branched side chains and are called the branched-chain amino acids.

    The nine marked essential
  3. 4.3

    Conditionally Essential

    The next six, arginine, cysteine, glutamine, glycine, proline and tyrosine, your body normally makes, but it can't always keep up, for example in premature babies, after serious injury or during illness. Some are made from essential ones: tyrosine from phenylalanine, and cysteine using the sulfur from methionine.

    The six conditionally essential
  4. 4.4

    The Non-Essential Five

    The last five, alanine, asparagine, aspartic acid, glutamic acid and serine, your body makes easily from glucose and other amino acids. Non-essential doesn't mean unimportant; it only means you don't have to eat them.

    The five non-essential
  5. 4.5

    What the Side Chains Do

    The side chains decide how a protein behaves. Oily ones like leucine and phenylalanine avoid water and end up inside a folded protein. Charged ones like lysine and glutamic acid face the water outside. Cysteine's sulfur can bond to another cysteine, a cross-link that holds shapes together, like the curls in hair.

    The table: what each essential one does
  6. 4.6

    Raw Materials

    Some amino acids are also raw materials. Tryptophan becomes serotonin and melatonin, and a little niacin. Phenylalanine becomes tyrosine, which becomes adrenaline and the pigment melanin. Histidine becomes histamine. People with the inherited condition PKU can't process phenylalanine, which is why products with aspartame carry a phenylalanine warning.

    In your body
  1. 5.1

    Primary

    A protein's shape is described in four levels. The primary structure is simply the order of amino acids along the chain, drawn here as beads. That order is set by your genes, and it decides everything that follows. Change a single amino acid and the protein can change: sickle-cell hemoglobin differs from normal hemoglobin by just one.

    Level 1: the bead chain
  2. 5.2

    Secondary

    Secondary structure is how short stretches coil or line up. The two common shapes are the alpha helix, a spiral, and the beta sheet, strands lying side by side. Both are held by hydrogen bonds: weak attractions between the C=O of one peptide bond and the N–H of another.

    Level 2: helix, then sheet
  3. 5.3

    Tertiary

    Tertiary structure is the whole chain folded into its final shape. Oily side chains tuck inside, away from water, and water-loving ones face out, with a few sulfur cross-links holding it steady. The shape decides the job: an enzyme's pocket fits only the molecule it works on.

    Level 3: the folded chain
  4. 5.4

    Quaternary

    Some proteins are teams of chains, and that's quaternary structure. Hemoglobin is four chains, each holding a heme group with an iron atom that grabs oxygen. Collagen, the most common protein in your body, is three chains wound into a rope.

    Level 4: hemoglobin
  5. 5.5

    Denaturing

    The folds are held by weak bonds, so heat, acid or whipping can undo them. That's denaturing. When egg white turns from clear to white, or milk curdles with lemon juice, the proteins are unfolding and tangling. The peptide bonds don't break, so the amino acids are all still there, and cooked protein is usually easier to digest.

    Note
  1. 6.1

    In the Stomach

    Protein digestion starts in the stomach. Stomach acid denatures the proteins, unfolding them so enzymes can reach the peptide bonds, and an enzyme called pepsin starts cutting the chains into shorter pieces.

    Mouth, then Stomach
  2. 6.2

    In the Small Intestine

    In the small intestine, the pancreas adds more enzymes, trypsin and chymotrypsin, and enzymes on the gut wall finish the job, down to single amino acids and some pairs and threes. These are absorbed into the blood and go first to the liver.

    Small intestine, then Into the blood
  3. 6.3

    The Amino Acid Pool

    From there they join your body's amino acid pool, a supply that cells draw on all day. Your body breaks down and rebuilds a few hundred grams of its own protein every day, far more than you eat, and reuses most of the amino acids. Nothing is stored as spare protein, though, so you need a steady supply.

    The amino acid pool
  4. 6.4

    What It Builds

    The pool builds muscle, skin, hair and the collagen in bone; enzymes; antibodies; carriers like hemoglobin; and hormones such as insulin. Only when there's more than you need for building are amino acids burned for energy, or turned into glucose or fat.

    The three boxes
  5. 6.5

    Urea

    Burning an amino acid leaves its nitrogen behind as ammonia, which is toxic. Your liver joins two nitrogens with a carbon and an oxygen to make urea, this small molecule, and your kidneys send it out in urine. That's why a very high protein intake means a little more water.

    Marker 1: the two nitrogens
  6. 6.6

    Too Little, Too Much

    Too little protein over time leads to muscle loss, slow healing, weaker immunity and thinning hair; in severe shortage, especially in children, swelling from fluid, a condition called kwashiorkor, and poor growth. Extra protein beyond your needs is burned or stored as fat; very high intakes are mainly a concern for people with kidney disease.

    In your body
  1. 7.1

    The Limiting Amino Acid

    Your body builds each protein from a full set of amino acids. If one essential amino acid runs short, building slows down, however much of the others there is. The scarcest one in a food is called its limiting amino acid.

    First box: Limiting amino acid
  2. 7.2

    Complete Foods

    Animal foods, meat, poultry, fish, eggs and dairy, are complete proteins: all nine essentials in good amounts. Among plants, soy is complete, and quinoa and buckwheat are close.

    The first four rows
  3. 7.3

    Incomplete Foods

    Most plant proteins are incomplete. Beans, lentils and peas are low in methionine. Grains, wheat, rice, oats and corn, are low in lysine, and so are most nuts and seeds.

    The last three rows
  4. 7.4

    Combining

    But their gaps are different, so they cover each other. Beans are rich in lysine and grains in methionine, which is why rice and beans, hummus with pita, or peanut butter on whole-wheat bread give a complete set. They don't have to be in the same meal; over the day is enough.

    Beans + grains, then Over the day
  5. 7.5

    How Much

    The US recommended amount for adults is 0.8 grams of protein per kilogram of body weight per day, about fifty-six grams for someone weighing seventy kilograms. Needs go up in pregnancy and breastfeeding, and growing children need more per kilogram.

    First box, then More at some stages
  6. 7.6

    Found In

    Good whole-food sources are meat, poultry, fish, eggs, milk, yogurt and cheese, beans, lentils, peas, soy foods like tofu and tempeh, nuts, seeds and whole grains.

    In your bodyFound in

  2. 8.2

    Worth Knowing

    A few extras. Protein powders are just protein from milk, peas or soy, without the rest of the food. Collagen is rich in glycine and proline but has almost no tryptophan, so on its own it's incomplete; gelatin is collagen that has been cooked. And scores like PDCAAS and DIAAS rate proteins by their essential amino acids and digestibility; milk, eggs and soy score near the top. That's protein. Try the quiz below.

Quiz

Tap a question to see the answer

1. How many kcal does a gram of protein give?

About 4, the same as carbohydrate (Figure 1).

2. Which atom do proteins have that fats and carbohydrates don’t?

Nitrogen, in the amine group of every amino acid (Figure 2).

3. What three parts do all amino acids share, and what part differs?

An amine group, an alpha carbon and a carboxyl group are shared; the side chain (R group) differs (Figure 2).

4. What is released when a peptide bond forms?

One water molecule: an OH from one amino acid’s carboxyl and an H from the next one’s amine (Figure 3).

5. How many amino acids are essential? Name three.

Nine: histidine, isoleucine, leucine, lysine, methionine, phenylalanine, threonine, tryptophan, valine (Figure 4).

6. Which essential amino acid contains sulfur?

Methionine (cysteine, a conditionally essential one, has sulfur too) (Figure 4).

7. What does cooking an egg do to its proteins?

It denatures them: the folds unravel, but the peptide bonds and amino acids stay (Figure 5).

8. Where does protein digestion start, and which enzyme begins it?

In the stomach, where acid unfolds proteins and pepsin starts cutting them (Figure 6).

9. What happens to the nitrogen when amino acids are burned for energy?

It comes off as ammonia, the liver turns it into urea, and the kidneys remove it in urine (Figure 6).

10. Why do rice and beans go well together?

Grains are low in lysine and beans in methionine; each supplies what the other lacks (Figure 7).

11. What is the US RDA for protein for adults?

0.8 grams per kilogram of body weight per day, about 56 g for a 70 kg adult (Figure 7).

12. Is collagen a complete protein?

No; it has almost no tryptophan (Figure 8).
