Module 2 · Water-Soluble Vitamins

The second micronutrient lesson covers the nine water-soluble vitamins: the eight B vitamins and vitamin C. Unlike A, D, E and K, they dissolve in water, are stored only in small amounts (except B12) and are easily lost in cooking. The B vitamins all become coenzymes, small helpers that let enzymes release energy and build new cells. Each is shown as a ball-and-stick model with what it does, what happens with too little or too much, and where to find it in whole foods, including the difference between folate and folic acid. The figure is on the left and the lesson script on the right; the quiz is at the end.

  2. 1.2

    The B Vitamins

    The B vitamins are grouped by what they help with. B1, B2 and B3, and B5 and B7, help release energy from food. B6 and folate help build amino acids, DNA and new cells. B12 keeps blood and nerves healthy. Each box links to its figure.

    The B-vitamin branch, left to right
  3. 1.3

    Vitamin C

    Vitamin C, on the right, is needed to make collagen and works as an antioxidant.

    The right-hand branch
  4. 1.4

    Too Little

    The bottom row shows what happens with too little: beriberi and pellagra, anemia and nerve damage, neural tube defects in pregnancy, and scurvy. Pantothenic acid and biotin are in almost all foods, so shortages are rare.

    The bottom row
  5. 1.5

    What They Share

    All nine are covered in oxygen and nitrogen atoms, so they dissolve in water. Apart from B12, the body keeps only small stores and passes extra in urine, so a steady supply from food matters. And they're easily lost in cooking.

    The three boxes
  1. 2.1

    Enzymes Need Helpers

    Enzymes are proteins that speed up the reactions in your cells. Many can't work alone: they need a small helper molecule, a coenzyme, to carry electrons, carbon atoms or other groups.

    The first two steps
  2. 2.2

    Vitamins Become Coenzymes

    Each B vitamin is changed slightly in the body, often by adding phosphate, to make one of these helpers. Because coenzymes are reused, you need only milligrams or micrograms a day.

    The last two steps
  3. 2.3

    Each Vitamin, Its Coenzyme

    This table pairs each B vitamin with its coenzyme. Thiamin becomes TPP, riboflavin becomes FAD, niacin becomes NAD, pantothenic acid becomes coenzyme A, B6 becomes PLP, folate becomes THF, and B12 becomes two cobalamin coenzymes.

    The table, row by row
  4. 2.4

    No Energy of Their Own

    A common mix-up: B vitamins don't give you energy. They have no usable calories. They're the tools that release the energy in carbohydrate, fat and protein. A shortage makes you tired, but extra won't make you more energetic.

    The box
  1. 3.1

    Thiamin

    Thiamin, vitamin B1, is two rings joined by a short bridge. The left ring has two nitrogens; the right ring has a sulfur, in yellow. Two phosphates added at the OH make its coenzyme, TPP, which helps break down glucose.

    Markers 1 to 3
  2. 3.2

    Thiamin Facts

    Too little causes beriberi, with nerve damage, weakness and heart failure, and in heavy drinkers a serious brain disorder. Adults need about 1.1 to 1.2 milligrams a day, from pork, trout, beans, lentils, whole grains and sunflower seeds.

    In your body
  3. 3.3

    Riboflavin

    Riboflavin, B2, has three flat rings that can pick up and release two hydrogens. That's how its coenzyme, FAD, carries electrons. The rings also make it bright yellow. The chain hanging off is a sugar alcohol.

    Markers 1 and 2
  4. 3.4

    Riboflavin Facts

    Too little causes cracks at the corners of the mouth and a sore tongue. It's in milk, yogurt, eggs, almonds and mushrooms. Light destroys it, which is why milk comes in opaque containers.

    In your body
  5. 3.5

    Niacin

    Niacin, B3, comes in two simple forms: nicotinic acid, ending in COOH, and nicotinamide, ending in CONH2. Both make NAD, used in hundreds of reactions. Your body can also make niacin from the amino acid tryptophan. Too little causes pellagra: a rash in sunlight, diarrhea and confusion. Large supplement doses of nicotinic acid cause flushing. Good sources are tuna, chicken, peanuts, mushrooms and brown rice.

    Markers 1 and 2
  1. 4.1

    Pantothenic Acid

    Pantothenic acid, B5, is two small pieces joined by an amide link, like a peptide bond. The body builds it into coenzyme A, which carries two-carbon pieces in and out of the energy cycle and is needed to make and break down fat.

    Markers 1 and 2
  2. 4.2

    From Everywhere

    Its name means from everywhere: almost every food has some, so a shortage is very rare. Liver, chicken, mushrooms, avocado and eggs are rich sources.

    In your body
  3. 4.3

    Biotin

    Biotin, B7, is two fused rings, one with sulfur and one with two NH groups. The NH ring is where carbon dioxide is attached and handed on; the acid tail links biotin to its enzymes.

    Markers 1 to 3
  4. 4.4

    Biotin Facts

    Biotin helps make fat, glucose and some amino acids. A shortage is rare. Raw egg white contains avidin, which binds biotin, but cooking destroys it. Food sources include egg yolks, liver, salmon, seeds and sweet potato.

    In your body
  5. 4.5

    A Lab-Test Warning

    No harm is known from high biotin intake, but high-dose supplements can distort some blood tests, including thyroid and heart tests, so tell your doctor if you take them.

    Note
  1. 5.1

    Vitamin B6

    Pyridoxine, one form of B6, is a ring with one nitrogen. In the body, one CH2OH becomes an aldehyde and a phosphate is added, making PLP, needed by over a hundred enzymes, mostly for amino acids, and for making neurotransmitters.

    Markers 1 and 2
  2. 5.2

    B6 Facts

    Too little causes anemia, cracked lips and confusion. Too much, from supplements above a hundred milligrams a day for a long time, can damage nerves. Chickpeas, fish, chicken, potatoes and bananas are good sources.

    In your body
  3. 5.3

    Folic Acid

    Folate has three parts: a two-ring pterin, a benzene ring, and the amino acid glutamate. This is folic acid, the synthetic form added to flour and supplements. It's very stable and well absorbed.

    Folic acid, markers 1 to 3
  4. 5.4

    Food Folate

    The main form in food and blood looks almost the same, but the pterin carries four extra hydrogens and a methyl group. That's the active form, ready to hand on a one-carbon unit. Your body must add those hydrogens to folic acid before it can use it.

    The second molecule, markers 4 and 5
  5. 5.5

    Folate Facts

    Folate is needed to build DNA, so every dividing cell depends on it. Too little causes anemia, and before and early in pregnancy raises the risk of neural tube defects, which is why folic acid is recommended for anyone who could become pregnant. Too much folic acid can hide the anemia of B12 deficiency. Lentils, chickpeas, leafy greens and asparagus are rich in folate.

    In your body
  1. 6.1

    The Cobalt Vitamin

    B12 is the largest vitamin, about a hundred and eighty atoms, so it's shown as a cartoon. At its center is a cobalt atom, held by a ring of four linked five-membered rings, with a long tail looping underneath. The group on top changes: methyl or adenosyl in the body, cyano in most supplements.

    The cartoon
  2. 6.2

    Absorption

    Absorbing B12 takes several steps. Stomach acid frees it from protein; intrinsic factor, made by the stomach lining, binds it; and the pair is taken up at the end of the small intestine. The liver stores enough for several years.

    The steps, top to bottom
  3. 6.3

    Who Runs Short

    Only bacteria make B12, and it reaches us through animal foods, so vegans need fortified foods or a supplement. Many older adults absorb less from food. And people without intrinsic factor need treatment under a doctor's care.

    The three boxes
  4. 6.4

    B12 Facts

    B12 works with folate to make DNA and red blood cells, and keeps nerves healthy. Too little causes anemia and nerve damage that can become permanent. No harm is known from too much. Adults need 2.4 micrograms a day, from clams, liver, fish, meat, eggs and dairy.

    The facts
  5. 6.5

    Why It Matters

    Because folic acid can correct the anemia but not the nerve damage, a B12 shortage can go unnoticed when folic acid intake is high.

    Note
  1. 7.1

    Ascorbic Acid

    Vitamin C is ascorbic acid: a five-membered ring with a short side chain and four OH groups. The two OH groups on the double bond give up their hydrogens very easily. That makes it an antioxidant.

    Ascorbic acid, markers 1 and 3
  2. 7.2

    Oxidized and Back

    Once it has given them up, both carbons carry a C double bond O: dehydroascorbic acid. Your cells can turn it back into ascorbic acid.

    Marker 2
  3. 7.3

    What It Does

    Vitamin C keeps the enzymes that make collagen working, helps you absorb iron from plant foods in the same meal, and mops up reactive molecules.

    The three boxes
  4. 7.4

    Vitamin C Facts

    Humans can't make vitamin C, so it must come from food. Adults need 75 to 90 milligrams a day, more for smokers. Too little for about a month causes scurvy: bleeding gums, bruising and slow healing. Very high supplement doses can upset the stomach. Peppers, kiwi, citrus, strawberries and broccoli are rich sources.

    In your body
  1. 8.1

    Cooking Losses

    Vitamin C, folate and thiamin are damaged by heat and leach into cooking water. Steaming or microwaving briefly keeps more, and eating some vegetables raw helps. Riboflavin is damaged by light.

    The table
  2. 8.2

    Forms on the Label

    On ingredient lists you'll see thiamin mononitrate, niacinamide, calcium pantothenate, pyridoxine hydrochloride, folic acid and cyanocobalamin. Ascorbic acid is also added to foods to stop browning.

    The table, row by row
  3. 8.3

    Enriched Flour

    Refined flour loses B vitamins and iron in milling. In the US, enriched flour has thiamin, riboflavin, niacin and iron added back, and since 1998 folic acid as well.

    First box
  4. 8.4

    Daily Values

    Folate is listed in micrograms of dietary folate equivalents, which allows for folic acid being better absorbed. The Daily Values are listed in the last box.

    The last two boxes
  1. 9.1

    Where the B Vitamins and C Go

    Here's where the water-soluble vitamins are used: first inside a single cell, then everywhere else in the body. They work in every cell, and most aren't stored, so the kidneys remove any extra. Vitamin B12 is the exception: the liver keeps a few years' worth.

    The cell drawing and the list of places
  2. 9.2

    Inside a Cell

    Water-soluble vitamins don't build anything themselves. They're helpers that enzymes need to work. Most of the B vitamins team up with the enzymes in the mitochondria that turn food into ATP. Folate, with help from B12, supplies parts the cell needs to copy DNA. Vitamin C works in the rough ER, helping finish collagen.

    In food and in the body

    B vitamins. Spread across whole grains, meat, eggs, dairy and legumes; B12 comes only from animal foods or supplements.

    Folate. From leafy greens and beans, and folic acid in fortified flour; needed fast when cells divide, as in pregnancy.

    Vitamin C. From fruit and vegetables; without it, collagen is weak and scurvy follows.

    The cell drawing and its numbered parts
  3. 9.3

    Energy and Nerves

    B1, B2, B3, B5 and B7 are coenzymes that help every cell, like these muscle cells, release energy from food. Nerve cells depend on thiamin to burn glucose, and B6 and B12 help make neurotransmitters and myelin.

    In food and in the body

    Thiamin (B1). Whole grains, pork, beans and fortified cereals. In the body it is a coenzyme for burning glucose, which is why nerves and the brain need it most.

    In the list: brain and nerves, and muscle
  4. 9.4

    Blood and B12

    Bone marrow needs folate and B12 to make new red blood cells. The stomach makes intrinsic factor, which B12 needs to be absorbed at the far end of the small intestine, and the liver stores a few years' supply.

    In food and in the body

    Folate. Leafy greens, beans and fortified grains. It helps copy DNA, so fast-dividing cells like those in the marrow need it.

    Vitamin B12. Only animal foods, such as meat, fish, eggs and dairy, plus fortified foods. It works with folate in making red blood cells and keeps myelin healthy.

    In the list: bone marrow, stomach, small intestine and liver
  5. 9.5

    Vitamin C and the Exit

    Vitamin C is needed to make collagen, which holds the skin, gums and blood vessels together; the adrenal glands hold some of the highest levels. The kidneys remove the extra water-soluble vitamins, and riboflavin turns urine bright yellow.

    In food and in the body

    Vitamin C. Citrus, berries, peppers, kiwi and broccoli. It helps make collagen and helps absorb iron from plants.

    In the list: skin, adrenal glands and kidneys
  6. 9.6

    Simplified

    This is simplified. These vitamins work in every cell, so the list gives the places that need them most or store them, and each one's main job.

    Worth Knowing

    A few extras. Bright yellow urine after a B-complex pill is just extra riboflavin. Regular vitamin C doesn't prevent colds for most people. Quiz

Tap a question to see the answer

1. Why do the B vitamins and vitamin C dissolve in water?

They have many oxygen and nitrogen atoms that form hydrogen bonds with water (Figure 1).

2. Which water-soluble vitamin is stored for years?

B12, in the liver (Figure 6).

3. What is a coenzyme?

A small non-protein helper an enzyme needs to work; B vitamins become coenzymes (Figure 2).

4. Do B vitamins provide energy?

No. They contain no usable calories; they help release energy from food (Figure 2).

5. Which deficiency disease comes from too little thiamin? From too little niacin?

Beriberi; pellagra (Figure 3).

6. Which two vitamins contain sulfur?

Thiamin (B1) and biotin (B7) (Figures 3 and 4).

7. Why is milk sold in opaque containers?

Light destroys riboflavin (Figure 3).

8. How does folic acid differ from the main food folate?

Folic acid lacks the four extra hydrogens and the methyl group; it is synthetic, stable and better absorbed (Figure 5).

9. Why is folic acid recommended for anyone who could become pregnant?

It lowers the risk of neural tube defects such as spina bifida (Figure 5).

10. What metal is at the center of vitamin B12?

Cobalt (Figure 6).

11. Why do vegans need fortified foods or a B12 supplement?

Only bacteria make B12 and plant foods don’t provide reliable amounts (Figure 6).

12. Name three things vitamin C does.

Keeps collagen-making enzymes working, helps absorb plant iron, acts as an antioxidant (Figure 7).
