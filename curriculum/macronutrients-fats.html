  1. 1.1

    Fats Overview

    Fat is one of the three macronutrients, alongside protein and carbohydrates, and it's the most energy-dense: every gram of fat gives about 9 calories, while protein and carbs give about 4. In your body, fat is stored energy, it builds the membranes around every cell, it helps you absorb vitamins A, D, E and K, and some fats are raw material for signaling molecules.

    Word for wordWhole tree
  2. 1.2

    Triglycerides and Chains

    Almost all the fat in food comes as triglycerides: a glycerol backbone holding three fatty acids, and each fatty acid is a chain of carbon atoms. What makes one fat different from another is those chains, specifically whether they have any double bonds between carbons.

    Word for word
  3. 1.3

    Three Categories

    That gives three main categories. Saturated fats have no double bonds, so the chain is straight and packed full of hydrogen; they're common in butter, beef fat and coconut oil and, because they can pack tightly together, tend to be solid at room temperature. Monounsaturated fats have exactly one double bond, and polyunsaturated fats have two or more. Those last two are the two kinds of unsaturated fat, and each double bond usually puts a kink in the chain, which is why olive oil, canola and nut oils stay liquid.

    Word for word
  4. 1.4

    Omega Families

    Within polyunsaturated fats there are two important families, omega-3 and omega-6, named for where the first double bond sits when you count from the tail end of the chain. Your body can't make the parent omega-3, ALA, or the parent omega-6, linoleic acid, so they're essential and have to come from food: omega-3 from flax, walnuts and oily fish, and omega-6 from seed oils, nuts and poultry.

    Word for word
  5. 1.5

    Trans Fats

    Finally there are trans fats. A small amount occurs naturally in dairy, beef and lamb, but most trans fat in food was man-made by partially hydrogenating unsaturated oils, which straightens the chains so the oil turns solid. Trans fat raises LDL and lowers HDL cholesterol, which is why it's now banned in the US and limited in the EU.

    Word for word
  6. 1.6

    The Mix

    Every fat in food is a mix of all these types; we just label it by the one that dominates.

    Word for word

    Same Kind of Molecule

    Fats and oils are the same kind of molecule, a triglyceride, made of carbon, hydrogen and oxygen, and the only difference is how tightly the chains pack. We'll see why in Figures 3 and 4.

    Title and subtitle: same kind of molecule, different packing
  2. 2.2

    Fat Versus Oil

    The first image puts a fat above an oil. On top is tristearin, three straight saturated chains, which melts at about 72 °C, so it's solid. Below is triolein, three kinked chains with one cis double bond each, which melts at about 5 °C, so it's liquid. They're the same kind of molecule made of the same elements, carbon, hydrogen and oxygen; the oil just has six fewer hydrogens because of its three double bonds. So 'fat' or 'oil' only tells you whether it's solid or liquid at room temperature, about 20 to 25 °C. Real fats are mixes, too: coconut oil is mostly saturated and melts around 24 °C, so it's solid in a cool kitchen and liquid in a warm one. The next image shows why that shape matters, and trans fats, which we meet in Figure 4, are mostly man-made.

  1. 3.1

    What Is a Bond?

    What is a bond?

    A bond is the attraction that holds two atoms together in a molecule. In the fats we're looking at, it's mostly carbon sharing electrons with hydrogen or with another carbon, and that sharing is what builds the chains.

    Word for wordMeet the atoms, panel A: C and H share one pair of electrons (blue dots)
  2. 3.2

    How Bonds Work

    How does that work exactly?

    Atoms have electrons in shells around them, and atoms are happiest when their outer shell is full. A bond is when two atoms share a pair of those outer electrons so each one gets to count them. One shared pair is a single bond, which lets the atoms spin freely around each other. Two shared pairs is a double bond, which is shorter, stronger, and locks the atoms in place so they can't rotate. That's the whole trick behind saturated versus unsaturated fat: saturated chains are all single bonds, so they lie straight and pack tight. Unsaturated ones have double bonds that put a kink in the chain, and that's what keeps oils liquid.

  4. 3.4

    The Triglyceride

    Now the triglyceride image. Almost every fat you eat, and the fat stored in your body, is one kind of molecule called a triglyceride. The purple box on the left is the glycerol backbone, three carbons long, and hanging off it are three fatty acids. Each fatty acid starts with an acid head, the end with the red oxygens. On its own that head is a carboxyl group, COOH, and it reacts with one of glycerol's OH groups, giving off water, to make an ester link, the red bar that holds the fatty acid on.

    Purple glycerol box, red oxygens and red ester bars

    Carbon Tails

    The long part sticking out to the right is the carbon tail, a zigzag of carbons numbered from the head, C1, out to the far end, which is called the omega end. There are only three elements here: carbon makes 4 bonds, oxygen makes 2 and hydrogen makes 1, and the hydrogens are hidden to keep the picture clean. In the tails, one line between carbons is a single bond, while the orange pair with two lines is a C=C double bond, and a double-bonded carbon has room for fewer hydrogens. The top chain has no double bonds, the middle one has one, and the bottom one has two.

    Straight and Saturated

    Here are the same 18 carbons three ways, and this time the small white hydrogens are shown. On top is stearic acid, which is saturated: there are no double bonds, so every tail carbon holds as many hydrogens as it can, two each and three on the end, and the zigzag runs straight.     The Cis Kink

    In the middle is oleic acid. Between C9 and C10 there's an orange double bond, and two hydrogens are gone, which is exactly what 'unsaturated' means. The two hydrogens left on that bond sit on the same side, which is called cis, and that bends the chain, so it melts at about 13 °C.

  3. 4.3

    Trans Stays Straight

    At the bottom is elaidic acid, a trans fat. It has the same double bond in the same place, but the hydrogens sit on opposite sides, so the chain stays nearly straight and melts at about 45 °C. So trans fat behaves much like saturated fat, while plant oils and most natural fats are cis.

    Where Trans Fat Comes From

    As the grey note says, most trans fat in food was man-made, by partial hydrogenation of vegetable oil, which we'll see in Figure 7. Small natural amounts come from ruminant animals, cows, sheep and goats, so dairy, beef and lamb contain a little, and the main one is vaccenic acid.

  5. 4.5

    Saturated Fat in Your Body

    In the pink box, saturated fat is part of the structure of your cell membranes and a dense backup fuel stored in fat tissue. Your liver can build cholesterol from fat breakdown products, and cholesterol is the raw material for steroid hormones, so the hormones come from cholesterol, not from saturated fat directly. But eating a lot of saturated fat raises LDL, the 'bad' cholesterol.

    In your bodyPink box: Saturated fat
  6. 4.6

    Trans Fat in Your Body

    Trans fat is different: your body doesn't handle the straight trans shape like natural unsaturated fat, so it packs into cell membranes much like saturated fat. It raises LDL, the 'bad' cholesterol, lowers HDL, the 'good' one, and is strongly linked to heart disease, which is why industrial trans fat is now mostly banned in the US. Ruminant trans fat is eaten in much smaller amounts; at normal intakes the evidence of harm is weaker, but in high-dose trials it raises LDL too.

    One Double Bond

    Unsaturated fats are grouped by how many double bonds they have. On top is oleic acid, which is monounsaturated, 'mono' meaning one: it has a single cis double bond at C9, and it's the main fat in olive oil.

    Two or More

    At the bottom is linoleic acid, which is polyunsaturated, 'poly' meaning many, with two cis double bonds at C9 and C12; it's in sunflower, corn and other seed oils. Each extra kink lowers the melting point again, from about 13 °C for oleic acid to about minus 5 °C for linoleic.

    Monounsaturated Fat in Your Body

    In the pink box, monounsaturated fat, mostly oleic acid, is the main fat in your stored body fat, your energy reserve, and it helps keep cell membranes flexible. Diets that swap saturated fat for monounsaturated fat are linked to better cholesterol numbers, with lower LDL and a better LDL-to-HDL balance.

  1. 9.1

    Many Kinds of Triglyceride

    Not every triglyceride is the same. The glycerol backbone has three slots, and each slot can hold a different fatty acid: saturated or unsaturated, short or long, omega-3 or omega-6. Which chains sit in which slots is what makes one specific triglyceride different from another. With about twenty common fatty acids in food, that's already around eight thousand possible combinations, because twenty times twenty times twenty is eight thousand and the order of the slots matters, so in practice there are thousands of different triglycerides, and every food fat is a mix of many of them. Olive oil, for example, is mostly triglycerides with oleic acid in two or three of the slots, while butter mixes short, medium and long saturated chains.

    Word for wordPanel A: tristearin S-S-S, triolein O-O-O, the mixed P-O-L one; slots 1, 2, 3
  2. 9.2

    Breakdown and Release

    When your body is low on energy, for example between meals, during exercise or overnight, hormones like adrenaline and glucagon rise and insulin falls, and that switches on the fat-releasing enzymes in your fat tissue: adipose triglyceride lipase starts the job and hormone-sensitive lipase continues it. Together they snip a triglyceride apart into its pieces: one glycerol plus three free fatty acids. The cut happens at the ester links, where each fatty acid's carboxyl end, the part with the oxygens, was attached to the glycerol backbone; water is added at each link, so the acid head is restored. The glycerol is small and water-soluble, so it travels to the liver, which can turn it into glucose. The fatty acids don't dissolve well in blood, so they ride on a carrier protein called albumin to muscle and the heart, where they're burned for fuel. In a deeper or longer fast, the liver takes up more of them and turns part of them into ketones, a backup fuel even the brain can use. Inside the cell, the chain is chopped two carbons at a time, a process called beta-oxidation. The oxygen end is just the handle; the real energy payoff is in the long run of carbon-hydrogen bonds, which is why fat gives 9 calories per gram.

Quiz

Tap a question to see the answer

1. How many bonds does carbon make, and how many does hydrogen make?

Carbon 4, hydrogen 1, oxygen 2 (Figure 3).

2. What are the parts of a triglyceride, and which part of a fatty acid joins the glycerol?

A glycerol backbone plus 3 fatty acids. Each fatty acid's acid head (–COOH) joins glycerol through an ester link; the carbon tail sticks out (Figure 3).

3. Are fats and oils different kinds of molecule?

No. Both are triglycerides made of C, H and O. Oils carry more cis-unsaturated, kinked chains, so they melt below room temperature; "fat" or "oil" just names the state at about 20–25 °C (Figure 2).

4. What does "saturated" mean?

Every tail carbon holds as many hydrogens as it can, because there are no double bonds (Figure 4).

5. Why is butter solid at room temperature while olive oil is liquid?

Butter is rich in saturated fatty acids with straight chains that pack tightly. Olive oil is mostly oleic acid, whose cis double bond kinks the chain so it can't pack (Figures 2 and 4).

6. What does the "3" in omega-3 mean, and where do you start counting?

Count from the tail (ω) end, ω1 = last carbon; the first double bond starts at carbon 3 (Figure 6).

7. Which omega-3s come straight from fish or algae?

EPA and DHA. Plant ALA converts to them only poorly (Figure 6, pink box).

8. How is industrial trans fat made, and why does it behave like saturated fat?

By partial hydrogenation of vegetable oil, which flips some cis double bonds to trans (Figure 7). A trans double bond keeps the chain nearly straight, so it packs and solidifies like saturated fat (Figures 2 and 4).

9. Name a natural source of trans fat.

Milk, butter, cheese, beef or lamb: vaccenic acid made by bacteria in cows, sheep and goats (Figure 4, note).

10. Do steroid hormones come from saturated fat?

No. They are made from cholesterol, which the liver can build from fat breakdown products (Figure 4, pink box).

11. What happens to a triglyceride when your body needs energy?

Lipases (ATGL, then HSL) cut its 3 ester links, adding water at each, into 1 glycerol and 3 free fatty acids. Glycerol goes to the liver, which can make glucose from it; the fatty acids ride on albumin to muscle and the heart to be burned for fuel, and in a deeper fast the liver turns some into ketones (Figure 9).
