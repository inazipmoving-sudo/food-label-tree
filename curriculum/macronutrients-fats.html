  1. 1.1

    Fats Overview

    Fat is one of the three macronutrients, alongside protein and carbohydrates, and it's the most energy-dense: every gram of fat gives about 9 calories, while protein and carbs give about 4. In your body, fat is stored energy, it builds the membranes around every cell, it helps you absorb vitamins A, D, E and K, and some fats are raw material for signaling molecules.

    In food and in the body

    Vitamins A, D, E and K. These are the fat-soluble vitamins: A is in liver, eggs and dairy, and as beta-carotene in orange and green vegetables; D is in oily fish and egg yolks and is also made in the skin in sunlight; E is in nuts, seeds and plant oils; K is in leafy greens. In the body, A is needed for vision and immunity, D for absorbing calcium for bones, E protects cell membranes from damage by oxidation, and K is needed for blood clotting.

  2. 1.2

    Triglycerides and Chains

    Almost all the fat in food comes as triglycerides: a glycerol backbone holding three fatty acids, and each fatty acid is a chain of carbon atoms. What makes one fat different from another is those chains, specifically whether they have any double bonds between carbons.

    In food and in the body

    Triglycerides. Where they are found and what they do is in the "In food and in the body" note under 8.3.

    Glycerol. Glycerol is the backbone of every triglyceride, so it comes with all food fats and oils, and it is also added to some processed foods to keep them moist. In the body it is released when stored fat is broken down, and the liver can turn it into glucose.

    Word for word
  3. 1.3

    Three Categories

    That gives three main categories. Saturated fats have no double bonds, so the chain is straight and packed full of hydrogen; they're common in butter, beef fat and coconut oil and, because they can pack tightly together, tend to be solid at room temperature. Monounsaturated fats have exactly one double bond, and polyunsaturated fats have two or more. Those last two are the two kinds of unsaturated fat, and each double bond usually puts a kink in the chain, which is why olive oil, canola and nut oils stay liquid.

    Word for word
  4. 1.4

    Omega Families

    Within polyunsaturated fats there are two important families, omega-3 and omega-6, named for where the first double bond sits when you count from the tail end of the chain. Your body can't make the parent omega-3, ALA, or the parent omega-6, linoleic acid, so they're essential and have to come from food: omega-3 from flax, walnuts and oily fish, and omega-6 from seed oils, nuts and poultry.

    In food and in the body

    ALA and linoleic acid. Where they are found and what they do is in the "In food and in the body" note under 8.1.

    Word for word
  5. 1.5

    Trans Fats

    Finally there are trans fats. A small amount occurs naturally in dairy, beef and lamb, but most trans fat in food was man-made by partially hydrogenating unsaturated oils, which straightens the chains so the oil turns solid. Trans fat raises LDL and lowers HDL cholesterol, which is why it's now banned in the US and limited in the EU.

    In food and in the body

    Cholesterol. Cholesterol is found only in animal foods, such as egg yolks, meat, organ meats, shellfish and full-fat dairy; plants have none, and the liver makes most of what the body needs. It is part of every cell membrane and the raw material for steroid hormones, vitamin D and bile acids. LDL and HDL are the protein-wrapped particles that carry it in the blood: LDL delivers it to tissues and can build up in artery walls, and HDL carries extra back to the liver.

    Word for word
  6. 1.6

    The Mix

    Every fat in food is a mix of all these types; we just label it by the one that dominates.

    Same Kind of Molecule

    Fats and oils are the same kind of molecule, a triglyceride, made of carbon, hydrogen and oxygen, and the only difference is how tightly the chains pack. We'll see why in Figures 3 and 4.

    Title and subtitle: same kind of molecule, different packing
  2. 2.2

    Fat Versus Oil

    The first image puts a fat above an oil. On top is tristearin, three straight saturated chains, which melts at about 72 °C, so it's solid. Below is triolein, three kinked chains with one cis double bond each, which melts at about 5 °C, so it's liquid. They're the same kind of molecule made of the same elements, carbon, hydrogen and oxygen; the oil just has six fewer hydrogens because of its three double bonds. So 'fat' or 'oil' only tells you whether it's solid or liquid at room temperature, about 20 to 25 °C. Real fats are mixes, too: coconut oil is mostly saturated and melts around 24 °C, so it's solid in a cool kitchen and liquid in a warm one. The next image shows why that shape matters, and trans fats, which we meet in Figure 4, are mostly man-made.

    In food and in the body

    Tristearin. Tristearin is a triglyceride with three stearic acids; it is found in small amounts in beef and mutton fat and cocoa butter, and in fully hydrogenated oils. In the body it is digested, stored and burned like any other triglyceride.

    Triolein. Triolein is a triglyceride with three oleic acids and the most common single triglyceride in olive oil. In the body it is digested, stored and burned like any other triglyceride.

  1. 3.1

    What Is a Bond?

    What is a bond?

    A bond is the attraction that holds two atoms together in a molecule. In the fats we're looking at, it's mostly carbon sharing electrons with hydrogen or with another carbon, and that sharing is what builds the chains.

    Word for wordMeet the atoms, panel A: C and H share one pair of electrons (blue dots)
  2. 3.2

    How Bonds Work

    How does that work exactly?

    Atoms have electrons in shells around them, and atoms are happiest when their outer shell is full. A bond is when two atoms share a pair of those outer electrons so each one gets to count them. One shared pair is a single bond, which lets the atoms spin freely around each other. Two shared pairs is a double bond, which is shorter, stronger, and locks the atoms in place so they can't rotate. That's the whole trick behind saturated versus unsaturated fat: saturated chains are all single bonds, so they lie straight and pack tight. Unsaturated ones have double bonds that put a kink in the chain, and that's what keeps oils liquid.

    Word for wordPanels A to C: shared pair, single vs double bond, straight vs kinked chain

    Trans Stays Straight

    At the bottom is elaidic acid, a trans fat. It has the same double bond in the same place, but the hydrogens sit on opposite sides, so the chain stays nearly straight and melts at about 45 °C. So trans fat behaves much like saturated fat, while plant oils and most natural fats are cis.

  4. 4.4

    Where Trans Fat Comes From

    As the grey note says, most trans fat in food was man-made, by partial hydrogenation of vegetable oil, which we'll see in Figure 7. Small natural amounts come from ruminant animals, cows, sheep and goats, so dairy, beef and lamb contain a little, and the main one is vaccenic acid.

    In food and in the body

    Vaccenic acid. Vaccenic acid is the main natural trans fat, made by bacteria in the stomachs of cows, sheep and goats, so it is in milk, butter, cheese, beef and lamb. The body can turn some of it into rumenic acid, a conjugated linoleic acid, and at normal intakes it is not linked to heart disease the way industrial trans fat is.

  5. 4.5

    Saturated Fat in Your Body

    In the pink box, saturated fat is part of the structure of your cell membranes and a dense backup fuel stored in fat tissue. Your liver can build cholesterol from fat breakdown products, and cholesterol is the raw material for steroid hormones, so the hormones come from cholesterol, not from saturated fat directly. But eating a lot of saturated fat raises LDL, the 'bad' cholesterol.

    In food and in the body

    Steroid hormones. Steroid hormones such as cortisol, aldosterone, estrogen and testosterone don't need to come from food; the adrenal glands, ovaries and testes make them from cholesterol. They control the stress response, salt and water balance, and sexual development and reproduction.

    In your bodyPink box: Saturated fat
  6. 4.6

    Trans Fat in Your Body

    Trans fat is different: your body doesn't handle the straight trans shape like natural unsaturated fat, so it packs into cell membranes much like saturated fat. It raises LDL, the 'bad' cholesterol, lowers HDL, the 'good' one, and is strongly linked to heart disease, which is why industrial trans fat is now mostly banned in the US. Ruminant trans fat is eaten in much smaller amounts; at normal intakes the evidence of harm is weaker, but in high-dose trials it raises LDL too.

    Monounsaturated Fat in Your Body

    In the pink box, monounsaturated fat, mostly oleic acid, is the main fat in your stored body fat, your energy reserve, and it helps keep cell membranes flexible. Diets that swap saturated fat for monounsaturated fat are linked to better cholesterol numbers, with lower LDL and a better LDL-to-HDL balance.

    Essential Polyunsaturated Fats

    As for all polyunsaturated fats, the parent omega-3 and omega-6 are essential, so your body can't make them and they must come from food. Your body uses them to build signaling molecules called eicosanoids.

    In food and in the body

    Eicosanoids. Eicosanoids are not eaten; the body makes them on the spot from 20-carbon fatty acids, mainly arachidonic acid and EPA. They include prostaglandins, thromboxanes and leukotrienes, short-lived signals that control inflammation, blood clotting, blood vessel tone and pain.

    Omega-3 in Your Body

    In the pink box, omega-3 signals tend to calm inflammation, and DHA is a major building block of your brain and the retina of your eye. Plant ALA converts only poorly into EPA and DHA, so fish or algae are the direct sources. EPA and DHA are the EPA and DHA diagrams that follow ALA in the omega-3 row.

    Omega-6, through arachidonic acid, makes the signals for immune responses, inflammation and blood clotting, and those are useful jobs.     In food and in the body

    Arachidonic acid. Arachidonic acid is in meat, poultry, eggs and fish, and the body also makes it from linoleic acid. It sits in cell membranes, especially in the brain, and is the main raw material for eicosanoids, the signals for inflammation, immunity and blood clotting.

    Two Ends

    A fatty acid has two ends. The acid end, the COOH group, is carbon 1, and it's the end that joins glycerol. The other end is a CH3 group called the omega end. Omega is the last letter of the Greek alphabet, so it just means the far end.

    Desaturases Add Double Bonds

    Your body makes double bonds with enzymes called desaturases. A desaturase takes two hydrogens off a chain and turns a single carbon-carbon bond into a double bond, and each one works at a fixed spot, counted from the acid end.

    In food and in the body

    Desaturases. Desaturases are enzymes the body makes, mostly in the liver; they don't come from food. Each one adds a double bond at its own fixed position on a fatty acid chain.

  8. 6.8

    How Far Human Enzymes Reach

    Human desaturases only reach the first nine carbons from the acid end; that is called delta 9. That is enough to put a double bond at carbon 9 and turn stearic acid into oleic acid. On an eighteen-carbon chain carbon 9 is also omega 9, so omega-9 is not essential: your body makes it.

    In food and in the body

    Delta-9 desaturase. Delta-9 desaturase, also called stearoyl-CoA desaturase, is made in the liver and fat tissue. It turns stearic acid into oleic acid and palmitic acid into palmitoleic acid, and it is most active when the diet is high in carbohydrate.

    Out of Reach: Carbons 12 and 15

    In food and in the body

    Delta-12 and delta-15 desaturases. These enzymes are found in plants, algae and some microbes, not in humans or other animals. In plants they make linoleic acid and ALA, which is why seed oils, nuts and leafy greens carry these essential fats.

  1. 9.1

    What a Triglyceride Is

    First, a quick reminder of what a triglyceride is. It's one glycerol backbone joined to three fatty acids, and those three fatty acids are where the 'tri' in the name comes from. It's the main form of fat in food, and about 95 percent of the fat in your body, almost all of it stored in fat tissue. It's the same molecule you saw in Figure 3, with the glycerol backbone in the purple box and the three fatty-acid tails.

    In food and in the body

    Triglycerides and glycerol. Where they are found and what they do is in the "In food and in the body" notes under 8.3 (triglycerides) and 1.2 (glycerol).

    Intro text at the top of Figure 9; the molecule itself is in Figure 3
  2. 9.2

    Many Kinds of Triglyceride

    Not every triglyceride is the same. The glycerol backbone has three slots, and each slot can hold a different fatty acid: saturated or unsaturated, short or long, omega-3 or omega-6. Which chains sit in which slots is what makes one specific triglyceride different from another. With about twenty common fatty acids in food, that's already around eight thousand possible combinations, because twenty times twenty times twenty is eight thousand and the order of the slots matters, so in practice there are thousands of different triglycerides, and every food fat is a mix of many of them. Olive oil, for example, is mostly triglycerides with oleic acid in two or three of the slots, while butter mixes short, medium and long saturated chains.

    Word for wordPanel A: tristearin S-S-S, triolein O-O-O, the mixed P-O-L one; slots 1, 2, 3
  3. 9.3

    Breakdown and Release

    When your body is low on energy, for example between meals, during exercise or overnight, hormones like adrenaline and glucagon rise and insulin falls, and that switches on the fat-releasing enzymes in your fat tissue: adipose triglyceride lipase starts the job and hormone-sensitive lipase continues it. Together they snip a triglyceride apart into its pieces: one glycerol plus three free fatty acids. The cut happens at the ester links, where each fatty acid's carboxyl end, the part with the oxygens, was attached to the glycerol backbone; water is added at each link, so the acid head is restored. The glycerol is small and water-soluble, so it travels to the liver, which can turn it into glucose. The fatty acids don't dissolve well in blood, so they ride on a carrier protein called albumin to muscle and the heart, where they're burned for fuel. In a deeper or longer fast, the liver takes up more of them and turns part of them into ketones, a backup fuel even the brain can use. Inside the cell, the chain is chopped two carbons at a time, a process called beta-oxidation. The oxygen end is just the handle; the real energy payoff is in the long run of carbon-hydrogen bonds, which is why fat gives 9 calories per gram.

    In food and in the body

    Adrenaline. Adrenaline, also called epinephrine, is not something you eat; the adrenal glands make it from the amino acid tyrosine. It is the fight-or-flight hormone: it raises heart rate and blood sugar and tells fat tissue to release fatty acids.

    Glucagon. Glucagon is a hormone made by the pancreas when blood sugar falls, not something you eat. It tells the liver to release glucose and fat tissue to release fat.

    Insulin. Insulin is a hormone made by the pancreas when blood sugar rises after a meal; it is a protein, so insulin in food would simply be digested. It moves glucose into cells and tells fat tissue to store fat rather than release it.

    Adipose triglyceride lipase (ATGL). ATGL is an enzyme made inside fat cells, not something you eat. It makes the first cut, taking one fatty acid off a stored triglyceride.

    Hormone-sensitive lipase (HSL). HSL is also made in fat cells and makes the second cut; it is named for being switched on by hormones such as adrenaline. A third enzyme, monoglyceride lipase, usually makes the last cut.

    Glucose. Glucose comes from starch and sugars in foods like bread, rice, potatoes and fruit, and the liver can also make it from glycerol and some amino acids. It is the main fuel for the brain and red blood cells.

    Albumin. Albumin is the most abundant protein in blood plasma and is made by the liver; it is not the same as egg white's main protein, ovalbumin. Besides fatty acids, it carries hormones, calcium and many drugs, and it helps hold water in the bloodstream.

    Ketones. Ketones, mainly acetoacetate and beta-hydroxybutyrate, are made by the liver from fatty acids during fasting, very low-carbohydrate diets or long exercise; food supplies almost none. The brain, heart and muscles can burn them when glucose is short.

  2. 10.2

    Stored Fat

    Fat under the skin holds most of the store, as triglycerides packed into fat cells, and it also insulates and cushions you. Fat around the organs is a smaller store that cushions the gut and kidneys; when there's a lot of it, it's linked to heart disease and type 2 diabetes.

    In food and in the body

    Triglycerides. Almost all the fat in oils, butter, meat, nuts and dairy is triglyceride. In your body, fat cells store it and release fatty acids and glycerol into the blood when energy runs low.

  3. 10.3

    Fat as Fuel

    Between meals, fatty acids leave fat tissue and are burned for energy. The heart gets most of its energy this way, and muscles burn fat at rest and during long, easy exercise. The liver packs some fat into VLDL to send out, and in a long fast it turns fatty acids into ketones, which the brain can use.

    In food and in the body

    Fatty acids. Every food fat is mostly fatty acids, three to each glycerol. In your body they travel on albumin in the blood and are burned two carbons at a time for energy.

    Ketones. Food hardly contains them. Your liver makes ketones from fatty acids during a long fast or a very low-carb diet, and the brain, heart and muscles burn them for fuel.

    Numbers 3 to 5: heart, muscle and liver
  4. 10.4

    Fat in Every Cell

    Fat is also built into you. Fatty acids form the tails of the phospholipids in every cell membrane. The brain and the retina are rich in DHA, an omega-3, and myelin, the fatty wrapping that speeds nerve signals, is mostly lipid.

    In food and in the body

    DHA. Found in oily fish such as salmon and sardines, and in algae oil; your body makes only a little from ALA in flax and walnuts. It is concentrated in the membranes of the brain and retina.

    Numbers 6 to 9: brain, retina, myelin and membranes
  5. 10.5

    Signals Made From Fat

    Some 20-carbon fatty acids become eicosanoids, short-lived local signals. Prostaglandins help protect the stomach lining, and others control inflammation, pain, clotting and blood flow wherever they're made.

    In food and in the body

    Arachidonic acid and EPA. Arachidonic acid, an omega-6, comes from meat and eggs and is made from linoleic acid in seed oils; EPA, an omega-3, comes from oily fish. Your cells turn both into eicosanoids.

    Number 10: the stomach lining

    The Whole Family

    That's the whole module, so Figure 11 puts it all together. At the top is the triglyceride, the form almost all fat takes in food and in your body: a glycerol backbone holding three fatty acids by ester links.     In food and in the body

    Palmitic acid. Palmitic acid is the most common saturated fatty acid in food, in palm oil, meat, butter and cheese, and the body also makes it from extra carbohydrate. It is stored in body fat, built into cell membranes and lung surfactant, and in large amounts it raises LDL.

  2. 11.2

    Saturated

    Saturated fat has no double bonds at all. Every carbon in the tail holds as many hydrogens as it can, so the zigzag runs straight, the chains pack tightly, and the fat is solid at room temperature. Our example is stearic acid, eighteen carbons, which melts at about sixty-nine degrees Celsius. You find saturated fat in butter, cheese, beef and lamb fat, coconut and cocoa butter. Mainstream guidelines suggest keeping it under about ten percent of daily calories, because eating a lot of it raises LDL cholesterol.

    Left branch: Saturated, the straight stearic chain, then the e.g. line
  3. 11.3

    Unsaturated

    Unsaturated fats have at least one carbon-carbon double bond, and in food these are almost always cis, so each one puts a kink in the chain and the fat usually stays liquid. Monounsaturated means exactly one, and most of it is omega-9: oleic acid, the main fat in olive oil, avocados, almonds and macadamias. Your body makes oleic acid itself, so omega-9 isn't essential. Polyunsaturated means two or more, and that's where omega-3 and omega-6 come in, named by counting from the tail end. Alpha-linolenic acid, ALA, has its first double bond three carbons from the end, so it's an omega-3, from flax, chia and walnuts. Linoleic acid starts at the sixth carbon from the end, so it's an omega-6, from seeds, nuts and poultry. Those two are the essential fatty acids: your body can't make them, so they have to come from food. Your body can turn some ALA into EPA and DHA, but only a small fraction, so oily fish and algae are the direct sources. ALA, EPA and DHA are drawn side by side in the omega-3 row of Figure 6, and linoleic acid in the omega-6 row.

    Middle branch: Monounsaturated → Omega-9 (oleic), and Polyunsaturated → Omega-3 (ALA) and Omega-6 (linoleic); tap a molecule to enlarge it
  4. 11.4

    Trans

    Trans fat has the same kind of double bond as oleic acid, but its hydrogens sit on opposite sides, so the chain stays nearly straight and behaves like saturated fat. Elaidic acid is the classic example. Most trans fat in food was made by partially hydrogenating unsaturated oils, the dashed arrow in Figure 1, and small natural amounts come from dairy, beef and lamb. It raises LDL and lowers HDL, which is why industrial trans fat is now banned in the US.

    Right branch: Trans, the nearly straight elaidic chain; compare with oleic, bottom left
  5. 11.5

    Worth Knowing

    A few extras worth knowing. Fat gives about nine calories per gram, against about four for carbohydrate and protein and about seven for alcohol. Vitamins A, D, E and K dissolve in fat, so you absorb them along with the fat in a meal. Cholesterol is also a lipid, but it isn't a triglyceride at all: it's a sterol, built from four fused carbon rings. Your liver makes most of what you need, and in food it comes only from animal products. LDL and HDL aren't two kinds of cholesterol; they're the particles that carry it in the blood. And no food fat is just one type: olive oil is about three-quarters monounsaturated and butter about two-thirds saturated. The table sums up the fat types side by side. This is general education, not medical advice, and the quiz is right below.

  6. 11.6

    Lipids in the Body

    Lipids in the human body, by share of total body lipid. Reference adult: about 70 kilograms, with about 13.5 kilograms of lipid in all, most of it in about 15 kilograms of fat tissue. Triglycerides, about 95 percent, about 12.8 kilograms: stored fat, almost all of it in fat tissue, about 12 kilograms, with smaller stores in muscle, liver, bone marrow and blood. Phospholipids, about 4 percent, about half a kilogram: the double layer of every cell membrane, most of it in muscle, brain and liver. Cholesterol, about 1 percent, about 150 grams: mostly free cholesterol in cell membranes, about a quarter of it in the brain and nerves, plus some stored as cholesterol esters. Miscellaneous, each under 1 percent, under half a percent together, about 50 grams: glycolipids, mostly in the brain, free fatty acids, the fat-soluble vitamins A, D, E and K, coenzyme Q10, carotenoids, bile acids, steroid hormones, eicosanoids, and skin waxes and ceramides.

    In food and in the body

    Phospholipids. Phospholipids are in egg yolks, soybeans (as soy lecithin), organ meats and in small amounts in all whole foods, and the body makes its own. They form the double layer of every cell membrane and coat the lungs and the fat particles carried in the blood.

    Glycolipids. Glycolipids, lipids with a sugar attached, are in small amounts in milk, meat and plant leaves, but the body makes its own. They sit on the outside of cell membranes, especially in the brain and nerves, where they help cells recognise each other and help form myelin.

    Coenzyme Q10. Coenzyme Q10 is in meat, fish, organ meats and nuts, and the body makes most of its own. It passes electrons along in the mitochondria to make energy and works as an antioxidant in membranes.

    Carotenoids. Carotenoids are the orange, red and yellow pigments in carrots, sweet potatoes, tomatoes, leafy greens and egg yolk. Some, like beta-carotene, are turned into vitamin A, and lutein and zeaxanthin collect in the retina of the eye.

    Bile acids. Bile acids are not eaten; the liver makes them from cholesterol. They are released into the gut to break fat into tiny droplets for digestion, and most are reabsorbed and reused.

    Ceramides. Ceramides are in small amounts in foods such as eggs, dairy, wheat and soy, but the body makes its own. In the skin they help seal in water, and inside cells they act as signals.

Quiz

Tap a question to see the answer

1. How many bonds does carbon make, and how many does hydrogen make?

Carbon 4, hydrogen 1, oxygen 2 (Figure 3).

2. What are the parts of a triglyceride, and which part of a fatty acid joins the glycerol?

A glycerol backbone plus 3 fatty acids. Each fatty acid's acid head (–COOH) joins glycerol through an ester link; the carbon tail sticks out (Figure 3).

3. Are fats and oils different kinds of molecule?

No. Both are triglycerides made of C, H and O. Oils carry more cis-unsaturated, kinked chains, so they melt below room temperature; "fat" or "oil" just names the state at about 20–25 °C (Figure 2).

4. What does "saturated" mean?

Every tail carbon holds as many hydrogens as it can, because there are no double bonds (Figure 4).

5. Why is butter solid at room temperature while olive oil is liquid?

Butter is rich in saturated fatty acids with straight chains that pack tightly. Olive oil is mostly oleic acid, whose cis double bond kinks the chain so it can't pack (Figures 2 and 4).

6. What does the "3" in omega-3 mean, and where do you start counting?

Count from the tail (ω) end, ω1 = last carbon; the first double bond starts at carbon 3 (Figure 6).

7. Which omega-3s come straight from fish or algae?

EPA and DHA. Plant ALA converts to them only poorly (Figure 6, pink box).

8. Why is omega-9 not essential, while omega-3 and omega-6 are?

Human desaturase enzymes can only add double bonds up to carbon 9 counted from the acid end. That is enough to turn stearic acid into oleic acid, an omega-9, so the body makes it. On an 18-carbon chain the omega-6 and omega-3 double bonds sit at carbons 12 and 15, out of reach, so linoleic acid and ALA must come from food (Figure 6, lower diagram).

9. How is industrial trans fat made, and why does it behave like saturated fat?

By partial hydrogenation of vegetable oil, which flips some cis double bonds to trans (Figure 7). A trans double bond keeps the chain nearly straight, so it packs and solidifies like saturated fat (Figures 2 and 4).

10. Name a natural source of trans fat.

Milk, butter, cheese, beef or lamb: vaccenic acid made by bacteria in cows, sheep and goats (Figure 4, note).

11. Do steroid hormones come from saturated fat?

No. They are made from cholesterol, which the liver can build from fat breakdown products (Figure 4, pink box).

12. What happens to a triglyceride when your body needs energy?

Lipases (ATGL, then HSL) cut its 3 ester links, adding water at each, into 1 glycerol and 3 free fatty acids. Glycerol goes to the liver, which can make glucose from it; the fatty acids ride on albumin to muscle and the heart to be burned for fuel, and in a deeper fast the liver turns some into ketones (Figure 9).
