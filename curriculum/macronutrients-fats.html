  1. 1.1

    Fat is one of the three macronutrients, alongside protein and carbohydrates, and it's the most energy-dense: every gram of fat gives about 9 calories, while protein and carbs give about 4. In your body, fat is stored energy, it builds the membranes around every cell, it helps you absorb vitamins A, D, E and K, and some fats are raw material for signaling molecules. Almost all the fat in food comes as triglycerides: a glycerol backbone holding three fatty acids, and each fatty acid is a chain of carbon atoms. What makes one fat different from another is those chains, specifically whether they have any double bonds between carbons. That gives three main categories. Saturated fats have no double bonds, so the chain is straight and packed full of hydrogen; they're common in butter, beef fat and coconut oil and tend to be solid at room temperature. Monounsaturated fats have exactly one double bond, and polyunsaturated fats have two or more. Those last two are the two kinds of unsaturated fat, and each double bond usually puts a kink in the chain, which is why olive oil, canola and nut oils stay liquid. Within polyunsaturated fats there are two important families, omega-3 and omega-6, named for where the first double bond sits when you count from the tail end of the chain. Your body can't make the parent omega-3, ALA, or the parent omega-6, linoleic acid, so they're essential and have to come from food: omega-3 from flax, walnuts and oily fish, and omega-6 from seed oils, nuts and poultry. Finally there are trans fats. A small amount occurs naturally in dairy, beef and lamb, but most trans fat in food was man-made by partially hydrogenating unsaturated oils, which straightens the chains so the oil turns solid. Trans fat raises LDL and lowers HDL cholesterol, which is why it's now banned in the US and limited in the EU. Every fat in food is a mix of all these types; we just label it by the one that dominates.

  1. 2.1

    What is a bond?

    A bond is the attraction that holds two atoms together in a molecule. In the fats we're looking at, it's mostly carbon sharing electrons with hydrogen or with another carbon, and that sharing is what builds the chains.

    Word for wordMeet the atoms, panel A: C and H share one pair of electrons (blue dots)
  2. 2.2

    How does that work exactly?

    Atoms have electrons in shells around them, and atoms are happiest when their outer shell is full. A bond is when two atoms share a pair of those outer electrons so each one gets to count them.

    Word for wordPanel A again: the shared pair
  3. 2.3

    One shared pair is a single bond, which lets the atoms spin freely around each other. Two shared pairs is a double bond, which is shorter, stronger, and locks the atoms in place so they can't rotate.

    Word for wordPanel B: single bond rotates, double bond is locked
  4. 2.4

    That's the whole trick behind saturated versus unsaturated fat: saturated chains are all single bonds, so they lie straight and pack tight. Unsaturated ones have double bonds that put a kink in the chain, and that's what keeps oils liquid.

Quiz

Tap a question to see the answer

1. How many bonds does carbon make, and how many does hydrogen make?

Carbon 4, hydrogen 1, oxygen 2 (Figure 2).

2. What are the parts of a triglyceride, and which part of a fatty acid joins the glycerol?

A glycerol backbone plus 3 fatty acids. Each fatty acid's acid head (–COOH) joins glycerol through an ester link; the carbon tail sticks out (Figure 2).

3. Are fats and oils different kinds of molecule?

No. Both are triglycerides made of C, H and O. Oils carry more cis-unsaturated, kinked chains, so they melt below room temperature; "fat" or "oil" just names the state at about 20–25 °C (Figures 2 and 4).

4. What does "saturated" mean?

Every tail carbon holds as many hydrogens as it can, because there are no double bonds (Figure 3).

5. Why is butter solid at room temperature while olive oil is liquid?

Butter is rich in saturated fatty acids with straight chains that pack tightly. Olive oil is mostly oleic acid, whose cis double bond kinks the chain so it can't pack (Figures 3 and 4).

6. What does the "3" in omega-3 mean, and where do you start counting?

Count from the tail (ω) end, ω1 = last carbon; the first double bond starts at carbon 3 (Figure 6).

7. Which omega-3s come straight from fish or algae?

EPA and DHA. Plant ALA converts to them only poorly (Figure 6, pink box).

8. How is industrial trans fat made, and why does it behave like saturated fat?

By partial hydrogenation of vegetable oil, which flips some cis double bonds to trans (Figure 7). A trans double bond keeps the chain nearly straight, so it packs and solidifies like saturated fat (Figures 3 and 4).

9. Name a natural source of trans fat.

Milk, butter, cheese, beef or lamb: vaccenic acid made by bacteria in cows, sheep and goats (Figure 3, note).

10. Do steroid hormones come from saturated fat?

No. They are made from cholesterol, which the liver can build from fat breakdown products (Figure 3, pink box).
