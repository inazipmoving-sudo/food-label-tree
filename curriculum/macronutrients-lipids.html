Module 1 · Other Lipids

Fats are one kind of lipid. This lesson meets the rest of the family, one group at a time: phospholipids that build every cell membrane, cholesterol and plant sterols, sphingolipids and waxes, the eicosanoid signals your body makes from fatty acids, and the fat-soluble vitamins, then puts them all in one table. Every molecule is drawn at the same scale with every hydrogen shown. The figure is on the left and the lesson script on the right; the quiz is at the end.

  1. 1.1

    What a Lipid Is

    In the Fats lesson every molecule was a triglyceride or a fatty acid. Those are lipids, but lipids are a bigger family. A lipid is simply an oily molecule that doesn't dissolve in water. Drop one in water and it beads up or floats, the way oil does on soup. That one property is what ties this whole family together, even though the members look very different.

    Title and subtitle
  2. 1.2

    Seven Branches

    The tree has the same layout as the fat family tree from the last lesson, now with lipids at the top. Six branches come off the trunk to either side: phospholipids; sterols, like cholesterol and plant sterols; sphingolipids; waxes; the fat-soluble vitamins and carotenoids; and eicosanoids, signals your body makes for itself. At the bottom of the trunk is the seventh branch, the fats, or triglycerides, which are built from fatty acids, with the whole fat family tree from the Fats lesson hanging below it, unchanged. Tap any box to jump to the figure that covers it.

    The Lipids root, the six side branches (phospholipids, sterols, sphingolipids on the left; waxes, fat-soluble vitamins, eicosanoids on the right), then down the trunk to Fats (triglycerides: glycerol + 3 fatty acids) and its subtree
  3. 1.3

    Triglycerides Dominate

    Of everything lipid we eat, about ninety-five percent is triglyceride, the fat from the last lesson. The rest of this lesson is the other five percent, plus lipids your body makes for itself. Small amounts, but very big jobs.

    The Fats (triglycerides) box at the bottom of the trunk: about 95%
  4. 1.4

    Energy or Not

    Not every branch gives you energy. Only the lipids whose fatty acids you digest and burn in real amounts do: the fats, or triglycerides, and the phospholipids. Sterols, waxes, eicosanoids and the vitamins don't give you calories at all.

    Fats (triglycerides) and Phospholipids, then the other side branches
  5. 1.5

    What Counts as Fat on a Label

    That's why food labels treat them differently. Total fat, the macronutrient at nine calories a gram, is the triglycerides plus the phospholipids. Cholesterol gets its own line, in milligrams, because it has no calories. And vitamins A, D, E and K are micronutrients, listed with the vitamins; they get their own lesson in Module 2.

    The three boxes under the tree
  1. 2.1

    One Swap

    A phospholipid starts out just like a triglyceride, with the same glycerol backbone. But only two of the three slots hold fatty acids. The third slot holds a phosphate group, a phosphorus atom with four oxygens around it, and on the end of that, usually a small group like choline. This one, with choline, is phosphatidylcholine, better known as lecithin.

    Numbers 1 to 3: choline, phosphate, glycerol
  2. 2.2

    Head and Tails

    Look at the two ends. The phosphate and choline carry electric charges, so that end is drawn to water; we call it water-loving. The two fatty-acid tails are long carbon chains with hydrogens all around them, just like in a fat, and they avoid water. One tail here is straight palmitic acid; the other is oleic acid, with the cis kink you met in the Fats lesson. So one molecule has a water-loving head and two water-avoiding tails.

    The two side brackets, then numbers 4 and 5
  3. 2.3

    The Bilayer

    Now put millions of them in water. The tails try to get away from the water, and the only way to do that is to hide behind other tails. So the molecules line up in two layers, tails pointing inward to an oily middle, heads facing the water on both sides. That double layer is the membrane around every one of your cells, and around the compartments inside them.

    The cartoon: heads outside, tails meeting in the middle
  4. 2.4

    Why the Kinks Matter

    The kinked tails keep the molecules from packing too tightly, so the membrane stays fluid, more like olive oil than butter. Cells adjust the mix of straight and kinked tails to keep it that way.

    The kinked tail pairs in the cartoon
  5. 2.5

    Lecithin as an Emulsifier

    Because a phospholipid likes water at one end and oil at the other, it can sit where oil meets water and hold the two together. That's called an emulsifier. Egg yolk is rich in lecithin, which is why it holds mayonnaise together. Food makers add lecithin, usually from soy or sunflower, to chocolate, margarine and dressings; on an ingredient list in Europe you'll see it as E322.

    Back to the head: the part that grabs water
  6. 2.6

    Found In

    Phospholipids are in every food made of cells, so you eat some with nearly everything, but the richest sources are egg yolks, soybeans, liver and other organ meats. Your body also makes its own. Their fatty acids give energy and are counted in total fat on the label.

    In your body
  1. 3.1

    Four Rings

    Cholesterol looks nothing like a fat. Instead of chains on a backbone it has four carbon rings fused together, three with six carbons and one with five, making a flat, stiff plate. On the left end is one OH group, the only part that likes water. On the right end is a short branched tail of eight carbons. Count it all up and it's twenty-seven carbons, forty-six hydrogens and one oxygen.

    Cholesterol: numbers 1, 2, 3
  2. 3.2

    Why It Is Not a Fat

    Now compare it with the triglyceride below it, drawn at exactly the same scale. The fat has a glycerol backbone, three fatty acids and three ester links. Cholesterol has none of those things. And your body can't break the rings down to burn for energy, so cholesterol has no calories. Extra cholesterol leaves through the liver, in bile, either as it is or turned into bile acids.

    The triglyceride under cholesterol, same scale
  3. 3.3

    What It Does

    So why have it at all? Cholesterol tucks in between the phospholipids of your cell membranes and keeps them steady, not too stiff and not too runny. It's also the raw material for your steroid hormones, like estrogen, testosterone and cortisol, for bile acids that help digest fat, and, using a close relative of cholesterol in your skin and some sunlight, for vitamin D.

    In your body
  4. 3.4

    Where It Comes From

    Your body makes most of the cholesterol it needs, much of it in the liver, so you don't need to eat any. In food, cholesterol comes only from animals: eggs, meat, dairy and shellfish. Plants don't make it.

    Back to cholesterol
  5. 3.5

    Plant Sterols

    Plants make their own sterols instead, like beta-sitosterol. It's the same four rings and the same OH, with just an extra two-carbon branch on the tail. They're in nuts, seeds, vegetable oils, beans and whole grains. Because they look so much like cholesterol, they compete with it for absorption in your gut, so less cholesterol gets in. That's why some spreads add them, and they can modestly lower LDL cholesterol.

    Beta-sitosterol, marker 1: the extra branch
  1. 4.1

    A Different Backbone

    Sphingolipids swap glycerol for a different backbone called sphingosine. Sphingosine is itself a long, eighteen-carbon chain with two OH groups and a nitrogen near one end. One fatty acid attaches to that nitrogen by an amide link. Sphingosine plus one fatty acid is called a ceramide, the molecule on the left. Like a phospholipid, it has a small water-loving head and two oily tails.

    The ceramide: markers 1, 2, 3
  2. 4.2

    Skin and Nerves

    Ceramides make up about half of the oily material between the cells of the outermost layer of your skin, where they help seal in water and keep things out. Add a phosphate and choline head to a ceramide and you get sphingomyelin, a major part of myelin, the insulating wrap around many nerve fibres, and of other cell membranes too.

    In food and in the body

    Ceramides. Your body makes ceramides itself, starting from the amino acid serine and palmitic acid. Food has small amounts, in eggs, dairy, meat, soy and wheat. In your skin they help seal the barrier; inside cells they also act as signals.

    Sphingomyelin. Sphingomyelin is in eggs, milk and dairy foods, meat and fish, and your body makes its own. It is a major part of myelin, the insulating wrap around nerve fibres, and of your cell membranes.

    In your bodyThe ceramide, then the sphingomyelin below it: the same ceramide with a phosphate-choline head (markers 1 and 2 on the sphingomyelin)
  3. 4.3

    Where They Come From

    Your body makes the sphingolipids it needs. Food has small amounts, in milk, eggs and meat, but they aren't a meaningful source of energy.

    Ceramide again
  4. 4.4

    Waxes

    A wax is even simpler: one fatty acid joined by an ester link, the same link that holds fatty acids to glycerol, to a long-chain alcohol, a long carbon chain ending in an OH. This one is cetyl palmitate: palmitic acid joined to a sixteen-carbon alcohol. Two long oily chains and almost nothing that likes water, which makes waxes very good at keeping water out.

    The wax on the right: markers A, B, C
  5. 4.5

    Waxes in Food

    Plants coat their leaves and fruit in wax to hold in water; the shine on an apple and the bloom on kale are wax. Bees build with beeswax, and carnauba wax from a palm leaf is used to glaze sweets and fruit. Our digestive enzymes can barely break waxes down, so they pass through, giving essentially no calories.

    The wax again
  1. 5.1

    Two 20-Carbon Fatty Acids

    Eicosanoids aren't something you eat. Your cells make them, on the spot, from two fatty acids that are twenty carbons long; eicosa means twenty. One is arachidonic acid, an omega-6, with four cis double bonds. The other is EPA, an omega-3, with five. Remember from the Fats lesson, Figure 6, that omega numbers count from the tail end: arachidonic acid's last double bond is six carbons from the end, EPA's is three.

    Arachidonic acid, then EPA; the omega markers
  2. 5.2

    Where They Come From

    Your body can make arachidonic acid from linoleic acid, the essential omega-6, and it's also in meat and eggs. EPA comes mostly straight from oily fish and algae; your body makes only a little from ALA, the essential omega-3, as we saw in the Fats lesson.

    Back to the two starting fatty acids
  3. 5.3

    Folded Into Signals

    When a cell needs to send a signal, enzymes cut one of these fatty acids out of a membrane phospholipid, then fold and oxidize it. Prostaglandin E2, at the bottom, is arachidonic acid with a five-carbon ring closed in the middle and extra oxygens added. These signals act close by and only last seconds to minutes.

    The arrow, then prostaglandin E2
  4. 5.4

    Three Families

    There are three main families. Prostaglandins help cause inflammation, pain and fever, protect the stomach lining, and widen or narrow blood vessels. Thromboxanes make platelets clump to form a clot. Leukotrienes call in white blood cells and can tighten the airways, which is part of asthma. Common painkillers like aspirin and ibuprofen work by blocking the COX enzymes that make prostaglandins and thromboxanes.

    The table
  5. 5.5

    Omega-6 Versus Omega-3

    Here's why the starting fatty acid matters. In general, the eicosanoids made from arachidonic acid, the omega-6, are stronger signals for inflammation and clotting, and the ones made from EPA, the omega-3, are weaker or calm things down. That's a simplification, since both kinds are needed, but it's one reason omega-3 fats get attention.

    Note
  1. 6.1

    Vitamins That Are Lipids

    Four vitamins, A, D, E and K, are lipids too. This is retinol, vitamin A: a ring and a chain with alternating double bonds, and one OH at the end. Like cholesterol, it's built from five-carbon units rather than from fatty acids, and that single OH isn't enough to make it dissolve in water.

    Retinol: markers 1, 2, 3
  2. 6.2

    Carotenoids

    Carotenoids are the orange and red pigments in plants. Beta-carotene is basically two vitamin A molecules joined end to end, and your body can split it to make vitamin A. Lycopene, the red in tomatoes, is a carotenoid that doesn't become vitamin A.

    The Carotenoids row
  3. 6.3

    Eat Them With Fat

    Because they're lipids, these vitamins and carotenoids are absorbed along with the fat in a meal, so a little fat in the meal helps. They are micronutrients, needed in tiny amounts and with no calories, and they get their own lesson in Module 2.

    The table: what each one does and where it is found
  4. 6.4

    Vitamin D

    Now the other vitamins up close, at the same scale. This is vitamin D3, cholecalciferol. Compare it with cholesterol in Figure 3: it's the same skeleton, with the same OH on the left and the same eight-carbon tail on the right, but the second ring has been broken open. That happens in your skin: sunlight's UVB rays snap that ring open in a close relative of cholesterol, and the molecule then settles into vitamin D3. Your liver and kidneys then convert it into the active form, which helps you absorb calcium and phosphorus for your bones. In food, D3 comes from oily fish like salmon and sardines, egg yolks, and fortified milk. Mushrooms and yeast make a slightly different form, D2 or ergocalciferol, from their own sterol when exposed to UV light; it has one extra double bond and one extra methyl group in the tail, and your body uses both.

    Vitamin D3: marker 1, the opened ring, then markers 2 and 3
  5. 6.5

    Vitamin E

    This is vitamin E, alpha-tocopherol. On the left is a double ring called a chromanol, with an OH group on it. That OH is the working end: it can give up its hydrogen to a free radical, a reactive molecule that would otherwise damage the fatty acids in your cell membranes, and that stops the damage spreading. The long saturated tail on the right, sixteen carbons counting its three methyl branches, anchors the vitamin among those fatty acids in the membrane, right where it's needed. Vitamin E is richest in nuts and seeds like almonds, sunflower seeds and hazelnuts, in vegetable oils such as sunflower and safflower oil, and in smaller amounts in leafy greens.

    Vitamin E: marker 1, the OH; marker 2, the chromanol ring; marker 3, the tail
  6. 6.6

    Vitamin K

    Vitamin K comes in two main forms, and both have the same head: a double ring called a naphthoquinone, with two C=O groups. What differs is the tail. Vitamin K1, phylloquinone, on top, has a mostly saturated tail, much like vitamin E's, with just one double bond. Plants make it, so it comes from leafy greens like kale, spinach and broccoli, and from vegetable oils such as soybean and canola. Vitamin K2, the menaquinones, have a tail of repeating units, each with its own double bond. The one shown is MK-4, with four units; your body makes some MK-4 from K1, and it's in meat, egg yolk and dairy. Longer forms, like MK-7, are made by bacteria and are found in fermented foods like natto and some cheeses. Either way, vitamin K's job is to switch on proteins that make blood clot, and others that work in bone.

    Vitamin K: marker 1, the shared ring; marker 2, the K1 tail; marker 3, the MK-4 tail
  7. 6.7

    Beta-carotene and Lycopene

    Last, the two carotenoids, drawn in full. Both have exactly the same formula, forty carbons and fifty-six hydrogens; they're just arranged differently. Beta-carotene has a ring at each end, and each half is basically a vitamin A molecule. An enzyme in your gut can cut it right in the middle, at marker 1, giving vitamin A, which is why beta-carotene counts as provitamin A. Its long run of alternating double bonds absorbs blue light, which is why carrots, sweet potatoes and pumpkin look orange. Lycopene has the same kind of chain, but its ends stay open instead of closing into rings, so your body can't make vitamin A from it. It's the red of tomatoes, watermelon and pink grapefruit, and it's absorbed better from cooked tomatoes eaten with a little fat.

    Beta-carotene: marker 1, the centre, then the rings; then lycopene, its open ends
  1. 7.1

    Where Other Lipids Go

    Here's where the other lipids are used: first inside a single cell, then everywhere else in the body. They aren't stored for energy like fat. Instead they build membranes and are made into hormones, bile, vitamin D and signals.

    The cell drawing and the list of places
  2. 7.2

    Inside a Cell

    Lipids are what the cell's membranes are made of. Phospholipids line up tail to tail to make the membrane, with cholesterol tucked in to keep it steady. The smooth ER makes these lipids, and in hormone glands it works with the mitochondria to turn cholesterol into steroid hormones. Those hormones then travel to the nucleus of other cells and change which genes are switched on.

    In food and in the body

    Cholesterol. Found only in animal foods; the liver makes most of the body's supply anyway.

    Lecithin. A phospholipid (phosphatidylcholine) in egg yolk and soy, also used as an emulsifier; the same molecule is in your membranes.

    Plant sterols. In nuts, seeds and fortified spreads; they compete with cholesterol for absorption in the gut and are barely absorbed themselves.

    The cell drawing and its numbered parts
  3. 7.3

    Membranes

    Phospholipids and cholesterol make the membrane of every cell in the body. In the lungs, a phospholipid film called surfactant coats the tiny air sacs and stops them collapsing when you breathe out.

    In food and in the body

    Phospholipids. Egg yolk and soy are rich in them, often sold as lecithin. In your body they form every cell membrane, help bile mix fat with water and make lung surfactant.

    In the list: cell membranes and lungs
  4. 7.4

    Cholesterol at Work

    The liver makes most of your cholesterol and sends it out in lipoproteins; it also turns some into bile acids, stored in the gallbladder. The brain makes its own cholesterol, about a quarter of the body's total. The adrenal glands turn cholesterol into steroid hormones, and in sunlight the skin makes vitamin D from a close relative.

    In food and in the body

    Cholesterol. Only animal foods contain it: eggs, meat, shellfish and dairy. Your body makes most of what it needs and uses it in membranes and to make steroid hormones, bile acids and vitamin D.

    Cortisol. Not something you eat: your adrenal glands make it from cholesterol. It raises blood sugar and helps the body handle stress.

    In the list: liver, gallbladder, brain, myelin and adrenal glands
  5. 7.5

    Sphingolipids and Signals

    Myelin, the wrapping on nerves, is rich in sphingolipids such as sphingomyelin. Ceramides help seal the skin's water barrier. And in the blood, platelets make thromboxane, an eicosanoid, to start a clot.

    In food and in the body

    Ceramides. Small amounts come from eggs, dairy, wheat and soy. In your body they are part of the skin barrier, together with cholesterol and fatty acids.

    Thromboxane. Not in food: platelets make it from arachidonic acid. Aspirin blocks its production, which is why it thins the blood.

    In the list: myelin, skin and blood
  6. 7.6

    Simplified

    This is simplified. Every cell uses these lipids, and the ovaries and testes also make steroid hormones from cholesterol, so the list gives each place's main job, not every detail.

    Only Fatty Acids Give Energy

    Read down the Energy column and one pattern stands out. Only the lipids whose fatty acids you digest and burn in real amounts give you energy: triglycerides, by far the biggest, and phospholipids. Everything else is for building, signalling and protecting.

  3. 8.3

    Two-Sided Molecules

    The second pattern is shape. Phospholipids, cholesterol and ceramides each have a small water-loving part and a big oily part. That two-sided shape is what lets them build the membranes around your cells and the barrier in your skin. That's the lipid family. Try the quiz below.

Quiz

Tap a question to see the answer

1. What makes a molecule a lipid?

It is oily and doesn’t dissolve in water (Figure 1). Fats are one kind of lipid.

2. About what share of the lipids we eat are triglycerides?

About 95% (Figure 1).

3. How is a phospholipid different from a triglyceride?

It has 2 fatty acids instead of 3; the third glycerol slot holds a phosphate head, often with choline on it (Figure 2).

4. Why do phospholipids form a double layer in water?

Their heads are water-loving and their tails water-avoiding, so the tails hide in the middle and the heads face the water on both sides (Figure 2).

5. What is lecithin, and why is it added to food?

Phosphatidylcholine (or a phospholipid mix) from egg yolk, soy or sunflower; it is an emulsifier, E322, that keeps oil and water mixed (Figure 2).

6. Why isn’t cholesterol a fat?

It has no glycerol, no fatty acids and no ester links: it is four fused rings with one OH. It isn’t burned for energy, so it has no calories (Figure 3).

7. Name three things your body makes from cholesterol.

Steroid hormones (estrogen, testosterone, cortisol), vitamin D and bile acids; it also goes into cell membranes (Figure 3).

8. Which foods contain cholesterol, and how do plant sterols differ?

Only animal foods. Plant sterols such as beta-sitosterol, in nuts, seeds and oils, look almost the same and compete with cholesterol for absorption (Figure 3).

9. What do ceramides do, and what is a wax made of?

Ceramides help seal the skin barrier; a wax is a fatty acid joined by an ester link to a long-chain alcohol, and we can’t digest it (Figure 4).

10. Which fatty acids are eicosanoids made from?

The 20-carbon fatty acids arachidonic acid (omega-6) and EPA (omega-3) (Figure 5).

11. Why do vitamins A, D, E and K need some fat in the meal?

They are lipids, so they are absorbed along with fat (Figure 6).
