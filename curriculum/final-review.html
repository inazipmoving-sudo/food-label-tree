  1. 1.1

    One Chart

    This is the final review, and it starts with the whole course on one chart. Everything in food falls into three branches, one for each of the first three modules and the same three that the Food Label Tree in Module 5 is built on, and every line on a label belongs to one of them.

    Title, then the root box
  2. 1.2

    Macronutrients

    On the left are the macronutrients, the things you eat in grams: fats, the other lipids like cholesterol, protein, carbohydrates, and water and ethanol. They supply the calories.

    First column
  3. 1.3

    Micronutrients

    Next are the micronutrients, eaten in milligrams and micrograms: the fat-soluble vitamins A, D, E and K, the B vitamins and C, and the major and trace minerals.

    Second column
  4. 1.4

    Non-Nutrients

    The third branch is the non-nutrients, Module 3: things in food, or done to food, that aren't nutrients. Additives, processing and flavorings are what people add or change. Antinutrients and bioactives are plant compounds that simply come with the food. Each box links to its lesson.

    Third column
  5. 1.5

    Where They Show Up

    Here's the key idea for the rest of this lesson. The first two branches are counted on the Nutrition Facts panel. In the third, additives, processing and flavorings show up in the ingredient list, while antinutrients and bioactives are never printed at all. You find those by knowing the food.

    The three boxes
  1. 2.1

    Calories

    Let's walk down a Nutrition Facts panel. Calories come from the macronutrients: about four per gram of protein and carbohydrate, nine per gram of fat, and seven per gram of alcohol.

    Row 1
  2. 2.2

    Fat, Cholesterol, Sodium

    Total fat, saturated fat and trans fat are the fats lesson. Cholesterol is a sterol, not a fat, so it gets its own line in milligrams. Sodium is a major mineral, and the salt is about two and a half times that number.

    Rows 2 to 4
  3. 2.3

    Carbs, Protein, Micronutrients

    Total carbohydrate, fiber and sugars are the carbohydrate lesson, protein is protein, and the four required micronutrients are vitamin D, calcium, iron and potassium. On the Daily Value column, five percent or less is low and twenty percent or more is high.

    Rows 5 to 8
  4. 2.4

    The Ingredient List

    Then the ingredient list. It runs from most to least by weight, and it's where additives, flavor lines and processing words like cultured, enriched or rolled appear.

    First three boxes
  5. 2.5

    What Is Not Printed

    Antinutrients and bioactives never get a line. Even a one-ingredient food can carry several of them, as we'll see in the next five foods.

    Last box
  1. 3.1

    The Label

    Our first food is plain whole-milk yogurt. These are typical values for one cup, for illustration, not any brand's label. The ingredient list is just cultured pasteurized whole milk.

    The whole label
  2. 3.2

    Sugars With None Added

    It shows eleven grams of total sugars but zero added. All of that is lactose, the sugar already in milk, which is why the added sugars line matters.

    Row 1
  3. 3.3

    Cultured and Pasteurized

    Cultured means bacteria turned some lactose into lactic acid. That acid sets the milk protein into a soft gel and gives the tang, which is sour taste from the flavorings lesson. Pasteurized means the milk was heated first to kill harmful microbes.

    Rows 2 and 3
  4. 3.4

    Fat, Protein, Calcium

    The fat is mostly saturated milk fat, and cholesterol has its own line. The protein is casein and whey, a complete protein rich in leucine. And one cup gives about a quarter of the calcium Daily Value.

    Rows 4 to 6
  5. 3.5

    The Molecules

    Here are the molecules: lactose, the lactic acid the cultures make from it, leucine from the milk protein, and the calcium ion. Because the cultures use up some lactose, many people who are lactose intolerant manage yogurt better than milk.

    Markers 1 to 4
  1. 4.1

    The Label

    Next, canned black beans, drained, half a cup. The ingredients are black beans, water, salt and calcium chloride.

    The whole label
  2. 4.2

    Salt and an Additive

    Four hundred milligrams of sodium is about a gram of salt, mostly from the canning liquid. Draining and rinsing washes some away, and no-salt-added cans have far less. Calcium chloride is an additive, E509, a firming agent that keeps the beans from going mushy.

    Rows 1 and 2
  3. 4.3

    Fiber and Protein

    The fiber is a quarter of the Daily Value in half a cup. The protein is rich in lysine but low in methionine, which is why beans pair so well with grains.

    Rows 3 and 4
  4. 4.4

    What the Can Handles

    The iron is plant iron, better absorbed with vitamin C in the same meal. And the lines that aren't printed: phytate, which binds some iron and zinc, and lectins, which the canning heat destroys. That's why canned beans are safe straight from the can, while raw or undercooked kidney beans are not.

    Rows 5 and 6
  5. 4.5

    The Molecules

    In the molecules you can see salt as sodium and chloride ions, lysine with its long side chain, phytic acid with its six phosphate groups that grip mineral ions, and the iron ion it competes for.

    Markers 1 to 4
  1. 5.1

    One Ingredient

    Rolled oats have a one-line ingredient list: whole grain rolled oats. Half a cup dry has no sodium, no added sugar, and four grams of fiber.

    The whole label
  2. 5.2

    Starch and Fiber

    Most of the twenty-seven grams of carbohydrate is starch, long chains of alpha-glucose. Part of the fiber is beta-glucan, a soluble fiber made of beta-glucose.

    Rows 1 and 2
  3. 5.3

    Rolled Is a Process

    Rolled is a processing word. The oats are steamed first, to stop a fat-splitting enzyme that would turn them rancid, then flattened between rollers. The protein is grain protein, low in lysine, so oats pair well with milk, yogurt or beans, and they keep their phytate-rich outer layers.

    Rows 3 to 6
  4. 5.4

    Alpha and Beta

    Here's the chemistry. Alpha-glucose and beta-glucose differ only in which way one OH group points. Starch is built from alpha-glucose, and your amylase enzymes cut those links into blood glucose.

    Markers 1 and 2
  5. 5.5

    Why It Is Fiber

    Beta links like this one resist your enzymes, so beta-glucan reaches the gut as soluble fiber and thickens into a gel. About three grams a day from oats, in a diet low in saturated fat, can lower LDL cholesterol, and the FDA allows a heart-health claim for it.

    Marker 3
  1. 6.1

    Almost Pure Fat

    Extra virgin olive oil, one tablespoon. Fourteen grams of fat times nine calories per gram is a hundred and twenty-six, and the label rounds it to a hundred and twenty.

    Calories and Total Fat
  2. 6.2

    Mostly Monounsaturated

    Ten of those fourteen grams are monounsaturated, mostly oleic acid, the omega-9 fatty acid. And zero cholesterol, because plants don't make it.

    Rows 2 and 3
  3. 6.3

    Extra Virgin

    Extra virgin is a processing term: the oil is pressed from olives without heat-and-solvent refining, so it keeps its vitamin E and more of its polyphenols, like hydroxytyrosol. Those polyphenols are also why fresh oil tastes peppery and bitter.

    Rows 4 to 6
  4. 6.4

    The Triglyceride

    Here's a triglyceride, the shape of every fat and oil: glycerol, shaded, holding three fatty acids. The kink at the cis double bond is what keeps olive oil liquid.

    Markers 1 and 2
  5. 6.5

    Vitamin E and Hydroxytyrosol

    Vitamin E dissolves in the oil and gives up a hydrogen from its OH group to stop fats from going rancid. Hydroxytyrosol has two neighbouring OH groups, and the EU allows a claim that olive oil polyphenols help protect blood lipids from oxidation.

    Markers 3 and 4
  1. 7.1

    Four Ingredients

    Last, a simple white sandwich bread: enriched wheat flour, water, yeast and salt, and a Contains line for wheat.

    Ingredients
  2. 7.2

    Enriched Flour

    Enriched means milling removed the bran and germ, so B vitamins and iron are added back: niacin, thiamin, riboflavin, reduced iron and folic acid. Folic acid has been required in US enriched grain since 1998, to help prevent neural tube defects.

    Rows 1 to 3
  3. 7.3

    Yeast and Crust

    Yeast ferments sugar into carbon dioxide, which raises the dough, and ethanol, which bakes off. In the oven, browning makes the crust's flavor and a little acrylamide, so toast to golden, not dark brown.

    Rows 4 and 5
  4. 7.4

    Salt and Wheat

    Bread isn't very salty per slice, but people eat it so often that it's one of the biggest sources of sodium in many diets. And wheat is a major allergen; its gluten proteins form the stretchy network that traps the gas.

    Rows 6 and 7
  5. 7.5

    The Molecules

    The molecules: folic acid, the stable added form of folate; ethanol and carbon dioxide, the two things yeast makes from each glucose; and acrylamide, which forms from the amino acid asparagine and sugar in dry heat.

    Markers 1 to 3
  1. 8.1

    Five Foods

    Here are all five foods in one table. Notice that even single-ingredient foods touch half the tree, and that each one has something important that isn't on the label at all.

    The table
  2. 8.2

    Worth Knowing

    A few last things. Labels round, so zero grams of trans fat can still mean a little. Serving sizes are reference amounts, not advice. Many whole foods have no label, so use a database like USDA FoodData Central. And every number in this lesson is a typical value; always read the package in your hand.

Quiz

Tap a question to see the answer

1. How many calories does a gram of protein, carbohydrate, fat and alcohol give?

About 4, 4, 9 and 7 (Figure 2; Fats, Water and Ethanol).

2. A tablespoon of olive oil has 14 g of fat. Why does the label say about 120 calories?

14 g × 9 kcal per gram is 126, and labels round calories above 50 to the nearest 10 (Figure 6).

3. Plain yogurt lists 11 g of total sugars but 0 g added sugars. Where does the sugar come from?

Lactose, the sugar naturally in milk (Figure 3; Carbohydrates, Figure 8).

4. What makes yogurt thick and tangy?

Cultures turn lactose into lactic acid, which sets the milk proteins into a gel and tastes sour (Figure 3; Processing, Figure 6).

5. Why is cholesterol a separate line in milligrams rather than part of Total Fat?

Cholesterol is a sterol built from four rings, not a triglyceride, and it has no calories (Lipids, Figure 3).

6. A can of beans lists 400 mg of sodium per serving. Roughly how much salt is that?

About 1 g, since salt is about 2.5 times the sodium figure (Figure 4; Major Minerals, Figure 8).

7. What is calcium chloride doing in canned beans?

It is a firming agent, an additive (E509) that keeps the beans from going soft (Figure 4; Additives).

8. Why can you eat canned kidney beans straight from the can but not raw or undercooked ones?

The canning heat destroys the lectins that cause illness (Figure 4; Antinutrients, Figure 6).

9. Why do beans and grains make a good protein pair?

Beans are rich in lysine but low in methionine, and grains are the reverse (Figure 4; Protein, Figure 7).

10. Starch and oat beta-glucan are both made of glucose. Why is one fiber?

Beta-glucan uses beta links that human enzymes cannot cut, while starch uses alpha links that amylase breaks (Figure 5; Carbohydrates, Figure 6).

11. Why are oats steamed before they are rolled?

To stop a fat-splitting enzyme that would make them go rancid (Figure 5; Processing).

12. What does “enriched” mean on flour, and which added vitamin helps prevent neural tube defects?

B vitamins and iron are added back after milling; folic acid prevents neural tube defects (Figure 7; Water-Soluble Vitamins).

13. Where do the holes in bread come from?

Yeast ferments sugar into carbon dioxide gas, which the gluten network traps; the ethanol bakes off (Figure 7; Water and Ethanol, Figure 5).

14. Why toast bread to golden rather than dark brown?

Browning forms acrylamide, and darker toast has more of it (Figure 7; Processing, Figure 3).

15. Which fat-soluble vitamin does olive oil supply, and what does it protect?

Vitamin E, alpha-tocopherol, which protects fats in the oil and in your cell membranes from oxidation (Figure 6; Fat-Soluble Vitamins, Figure 5).

16. Which two Non-Nutrients lessons cover compounds that never get their own label line?

Antinutrients and bioactives, such as phytate and polyphenols (Figures 1 and 2).

17. Does “0 g trans fat” guarantee there is none?

No. Less than 0.5 g per serving can be listed as 0 g (Figure 8).

18. On the % Daily Value column, what counts as low and what counts as high?

5% or less is low and 20% or more is high (Figure 2).
