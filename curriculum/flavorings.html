Module 3 · Flavorings

Flavor is taste plus smell. This lesson covers the five basic tastes and the receptors that sense them, the umami molecules glutamate and inosinate, four common aroma molecules (vanillin, cinnamaldehyde, menthol and limonene), and what natural and artificial flavor mean on a label. Each molecule is shown in ball-and-stick form with what it does, the cautions, and where it is found. The figure is on the left and the lesson script on the right; the quiz is at the end.

  1. 1.1

    Flavor

    This lesson is about flavor and flavorings. What we call flavor is mostly two senses working together: taste on the tongue and smell in the nose.

    Title, then the root box
  2. 1.2

    Five Tastes

    Taste has five basic tastes, each with its own receptor: sweet, bitter, sour, salty and umami. Under each are the foods that trigger it.

    The left branch
  3. 1.3

    Smell and Labels

    Smell covers thousands of aromas, like vanilla, mint, citrus and cinnamon. And on the right are the flavor lines you see on a label: natural and artificial flavor. Each box links to its figure.

    The two right branches
  4. 1.4

    Pinch Your Nose

    Try it: pinch your nose and a jelly bean tastes only sweet. Let go, and the cherry or lemon comes back. That's smell doing most of the work.

    First box
  5. 1.5

    Molecules Meet Receptors

    Every taste and smell starts when a molecule fits a receptor protein on a sensory cell. Most added flavorings are aroma molecules; a few, like MSG, act on taste.

    The last two boxes
  1. 2.1

    Sweet and Umami

    Here are the five tastes. Sweet signals energy and umami signals protein, and they share a receptor part: T1R3 pairs with T1R2 for sweet and with T1R1 for umami.

    Rows 1 and 2
  2. 2.2

    Salty, Sour, Bitter

    Salty signals sodium, a mineral we need. Sour signals acid, from unripe or spoiled food. And bitter warns of possible poisons, using about 25 different receptors.

    Rows 3 to 5
  3. 2.3

    Smell

    Smell does most of the work. As you chew, aroma molecules rise from the back of your mouth into your nose, where about 400 kinds of receptor combine to tell a huge number of smells apart.

    First box
  4. 2.4

    Tongue Map Myth

    The tongue map you may have learned is a myth: every part with taste buds senses all five tastes. And taste cells are replaced every week or two.

    Second box
  5. 2.5

    Hot and Cool

    Chili heat, mint cool and the fizz of soda aren't tastes or smells at all. They're touch and pain nerves reacting to chemicals, as we saw in Bioactives.

    Third box
  1. 3.1

    Sucrose

    Table sugar is glucose and fructose joined through an oxygen. Its OH groups grip the sweet receptor's clamp-like pocket.

    Marker 1
  2. 3.2

    One Sweet Receptor

    There's one sweet receptor, built from two proteins, and sugars, sweeteners and even a few plant proteins all fit it in different spots. Sweeteners like sucralose fit it hundreds of times more tightly, so a tiny amount tastes as sweet.

    In your body
  3. 3.3

    Sweet Adapts

    Liking for sweetness falls when people eat less sugar for a few weeks. The WHO advises keeping free sugars below ten percent of calories.

  4. 3.4

    Many Bitter Receptors

    Bitter compounds like caffeine share no single shape, so we need about 25 kinds of bitter receptor to catch them. It's our most sensitive taste, because many plant poisons are bitter.

    Marker 2
  5. 3.5

    Bitter Tasters

    People differ. About a quarter are strong tasters of certain bitter compounds, through a variant of one receptor, and often find broccoli, kale and grapefruit harsher. Salt, fat, sweetness and cooking all soften bitterness.

    In your body
  1. 4.1

    Hydrogen Ions

    Sour is the taste of acid. Acids release hydrogen ions, which in water ride on a water molecule as hydronium. Sour cells sense them through a channel called OTOP1 that lets them in.

    Marker 1
  2. 4.2

    Citric Acid

    Citric acid, the sour of lemons, has three acid groups, each able to release a hydrogen ion. Acids are added to foods for tartness and to preserve them, though frequent acidic drinks can erode tooth enamel.

    Marker 2
  3. 4.3

    Sodium

    Salt taste is mainly the taste of sodium ions, entering salt cells through a channel called ENaC. Very high salt also switches on bitter and sour cells, which is why heavily salted food tastes harsh.

    The three ions
  4. 4.4

    Salt Substitutes

    Potassium chloride, in salt substitutes, tastes salty but also bitter and metallic, because potassium doesn't fit the sodium channel as well.

    In your body
  5. 4.5

    Less Salt

    The Daily Value for sodium is 2,300 milligrams, about a teaspoon of salt, and most comes from processed and restaurant food. Taste adapts: after a few weeks of eating less, lightly salted food tastes right.

  1. 5.1

    Glutamate

    Glutamate is one of the amino acids in every protein. Free, unattached glutamate is what tastes savory, and the umami receptor grips its second acid group. MSG is just glutamate paired with sodium.

    Markers 1 and 2
  2. 5.2

    Inosinate

    Inosinate, from meat and fish, binds the same receptor at a different spot and multiplies glutamate's effect. That's why dashi, or tomato with parmesan, tastes so rich.

    Marker 3
  3. 5.3

    Discovery

    The Japanese chemist Kikunae Ikeda named umami in 1908 after finding glutamate in kombu seaweed broth, and went on to make MSG.

  4. 5.4

    MSG Safety

    The FDA lists MSG as generally recognized as safe, and controlled studies haven't consistently reproduced the symptoms people blame on it. Your body handles it like the glutamate in tomatoes. And it has about a third as much sodium as salt.

  5. 5.5

    Found In

    Glutamate is naturally high in parmesan, tomatoes, mushrooms, soy sauce, miso, fish sauce, kombu, yeast extract and broth.

  1. 6.1

    Vanillin

    Vanillin has an aldehyde on a ring, plus the methoxy and OH pair of guaiacol, the smoke molecule from Processing. It's the main flavor of cured vanilla beans, though less than one percent of vanillin actually comes from vanilla.

    Markers 1 and 2
  2. 6.2

    Cinnamaldehyde

    Cinnamaldehyde is a ring, a double bond and an aldehyde. It's most of cinnamon's essential oil, and it switches on TRPA1, the receptor behind cinnamon's warm sting.

    Marker 3
  3. 6.3

    Cassia Caution

    Cassia, the common cinnamon in North America, also contains coumarin, which can harm the liver in large amounts. Ceylon cinnamon has very little.

  4. 6.4

    Menthol

    Menthol is a carbon ring with one OH. It switches on TRPM8, the receptor that senses cold, so mint feels cool at room temperature.

    Marker 4
  5. 6.5

    Limonene

    Limonene, from citrus peel, is a terpene built from two isoprene units. Its two mirror-image forms smell different: one of oranges, one more of pine.

    Marker 5
  1. 7.1

    From Rice Bran

    Microbes can trim ferulic acid, from rice bran, down to vanillin. Because it starts from a plant and uses fermentation, the result can be called natural.

    Marker 1
  2. 7.2

    From Petroleum

    Most vanillin is built from guaiacol made from petroleum. The vanillin is identical, molecule for molecule, but it must be called artificial.

    Marker 2
  3. 7.3

    Ethyl Vanillin

    Ethyl vanillin has one extra carbon, doesn't occur in vanilla, and tastes two to four times stronger. It's always an artificial flavor.

    Marker 3
  4. 7.4

    The Definitions

    In US rules, a natural flavor comes from a plant, animal or fermentation source; an artificial flavor is anything else. Natural and artificial versions of the same molecule act the same in the body. Natural describes the source, not the nutrition or the safety.

  5. 7.5

    Secret Recipes

    A natural flavor can be a mixture of dozens of compounds plus solvents and carriers, and the recipe doesn't have to be disclosed, although major allergens must be.

  1. 8.1

    Flavor Lines

    Here's what the flavor lines on a label mean. Natural flavors come from plant, animal or fermentation sources; artificial flavors are synthesized, and when they supply the main flavor, the front must say artificially flavored.

    Rows 1 to 3
  2. 8.2

    Vanilla and Spices

    Vanilla extract has a legal standard. Vanillin is artificial unless stated as natural. And spices can be listed as a group, except paprika, turmeric and saffron, which also add color.

    Rows 4 to 6
  3. 8.3

    Umami Lines

    MSG must be named. Yeast extract and hydrolyzed protein are natural sources of free glutamate, so a food containing them can't claim no MSG. Smoke flavoring is filtered smoke.

    The last three rows
  4. 8.4

    Front of Pack

    On the front, strawberry yogurt suggests real fruit, while strawberry flavored can mean the taste comes from flavorings, so check the ingredients. In the EU, natural vanilla flavouring must get at least 95 percent of its flavor from vanilla.

    The two boxes
  1. 9.1

    Where Flavorings Go

    Here's where flavorings are used: first inside a single cell, then everywhere else in the body. Flavor is built in the mouth, the nose and the brain. After that, the molecules are digested and cleared like any other part of food.

    The cell drawing and the list of places
  2. 9.2

    Inside a Cell

    Every flavor starts at a cell membrane. Taste cells have receptor proteins for sweet, umami and bitter, and channels for salt and sour. Smell cells in your nose have hundreds of kinds of receptors. Chili and mint even use the body's temperature channels, which is why chili feels hot and mint feels cool. Small differences in receptor genes explain why foods taste different to different people.

    In food and in the body

    MSG. Glutamate, the umami taste; the same amino acid found in tomatoes, cheese and your own proteins.

    Capsaicin. From chili peppers; feels hot because it opens the heat channel.

    Vanillin. The main vanilla flavor, natural or made; detected by smell receptors.

    The cell drawing and its numbered parts
  3. 9.3

    Taste, Smell and Feel

    Taste buds on the tongue sense sweet, bitter, sour, salty and umami. The nose has about 400 kinds of smell receptor, which pick up aromas from the front and from inside the mouth as you chew. The trigeminal nerve senses the cool of menthol and the bite of mustard.

    In food and in the body

    Vanillin. The main flavor of vanilla, natural or made in a factory. A smell receptor in your nose detects it; the liver then clears it like other food molecules.

    Menthol. In mint. It triggers TRPM8, the cold receptor, so mint feels cool.

    In the list: tongue, nose and mouth nerves
  4. 9.4

    The Brain

    The brain combines taste, smell, texture and heat into the single experience we call flavor. That's why food tastes flat when your nose is blocked.

    In food and in the body

    Aroma molecules. The small molecules that give food its smell, like vanillin in vanilla. As you chew, they rise up the back of your nose to smell receptors that signal the brain.

    In the list: the brain
  5. 9.5

    Digested and Cleared

    Flavor molecules are swallowed and absorbed like the rest of the food; the gut even has taste receptors of its own. The liver breaks most of them down, and the kidneys send the products out in urine.

    In food and in the body

    Flavorings. Natural and artificial flavorings are used in tiny amounts. Your gut absorbs them, the liver changes most of them so they dissolve in water, and the kidneys pass them out in urine.

    In the list: gut, liver and kidneys
  6. 9.6

    Simplified

    This is simplified. Flavorings are used in tiny amounts, so the list shows where they act, not a dose.

  2. 10.2

    Worth Knowing

    A few extras. Losing your sense of smell makes food seem tasteless even though taste works; herbs, spices and acidity can help instead of more salt and sugar. Researchers are studying possible extra tastes, like kokumi and fat. And the pleasure of one flavor fades as you eat it, which is why there's always room for dessert.

Quiz

Tap a question to see the answer

1. Which two senses make up most of what we call flavor?

Taste on the tongue and smell in the nose (Figure 1).

2. Name the five basic tastes.

Sweet, sour, salty, bitter and umami (Figure 2).

3. Is the tongue map, with sweet at the tip and bitter at the back, correct?

No; every area with taste buds senses all five tastes (Figure 2).

4. How many receptors sense sweet, and how many sense bitter?

One sweet receptor (T1R2 + T1R3) and about 25 bitter receptors (Figure 3).

5. Why do some people find broccoli and grapefruit very bitter?

They carry a receptor variant (TAS2R38) that makes them strong bitter tasters (Figure 3).

6. What do sour cells actually detect?

Hydrogen ions released by acids, entering through the OTOP1 channel (Figure 4).

7. Why do potassium chloride salt substitutes taste bitter?

Potassium does not fit the sodium channel well and also activates bitter cells (Figure 4).

8. What molecule causes umami taste, and what boosts it?

Free glutamate; inosinate and guanylate boost it strongly (Figure 5).

9. How does the sodium in MSG compare with table salt?

MSG has about a third as much sodium (Figure 5).

10. Which receptor does menthol switch on, and what does it normally sense?

TRPM8, the cold receptor (Figure 6).

11. Can the same vanillin molecule be natural or artificial?

Yes; the label depends on the source: from a plant via fermentation it can be natural, from petroleum guaiacol it is artificial (Figure 7).

12. Can a food containing yeast extract claim “No MSG”?

No; yeast extract is a natural source of free glutamate (Figure 8).
