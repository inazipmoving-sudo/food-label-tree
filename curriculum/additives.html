Module 3 · Additives and E-Numbers

Additives are substances added to food to do a job: color, preserve, thicken, emulsify, sweeten or season. This lesson groups them by job and shows the key molecules, from citric acid to sucralose, with how they are labeled and regulated in the US and EU. Ultra-processed foods appear here because that is where most additives are used.

  1. 1.1

    Additives

    Module three is about additives: substances added to food on purpose to do a job. This tree sorts them by that job.

    Title, then the root box
  2. 1.2

    Four Jobs

    There are four main jobs. Appearance, which means colors. Shelf life: preservatives and antioxidants. Texture: thickeners, emulsifiers and anti-caking agents. And taste: acids, flavor enhancers and sweeteners.

    The second and third rows, left to right
  3. 1.3

    One Example Each

    The bottom row gives one example of each, with its E-number, from curcumin, E100, to aspartame, E951. Each box links to its figure.

    The bottom row
  4. 1.4

    E for Europe

    An E-number is the code an additive gets once it passes a European safety review. The hundreds digit roughly tells you the job: one for colors, two for preservatives, three for antioxidants and acids, four for thickeners and emulsifiers, five for acidity regulators and anti-caking agents, six for flavor enhancers, and nine for sweeteners and glazes.

    First box
  5. 1.5

    Not All Synthetic

    There are exceptions, which is why we group by job. And many additives are ordinary food compounds: vitamin C is E300, and pectin from apples is E440.

    The last two boxes
  1. 2.1

    Added on Purpose

    An additive is added to do a technical job: to color, preserve, thicken, sweeten or stop clumping. Ordinary ingredients like flour, salt and sugar aren't usually called additives.

    First box
  2. 2.2

    Same Molecule

    Ingredients are listed by weight, so additives are usually near the end. And an additive is the same molecule whether it came from a fruit or a factory; what matters is the amount, and the food it's in.

    The last two boxes
  3. 2.3

    Citric Acid

    Our first example is citric acid, E330. Three acid groups give the sour taste of lemons and limes, around a central carbon with an OH.

    Markers 1 and 2
  4. 2.4

    In You and in Lemons

    Your own cells make citric acid every day: it starts the citric acid cycle that releases energy from food. Most added citric acid is made by fermenting sugar with a mold.

    In your body
  5. 2.5

    Rules and Labels

    In the US, the FDA approves additives, or they're generally recognized as safe, and labels list them by name. In the EU, labels give the job plus the name or E-number. Worldwide, the INS number is the same digits without the E.

    The table, row by row
  1. 3.1

    Curcumin

    Curcumin, E100, is the yellow of turmeric. Two rings are joined by a long chain of alternating single and double bonds. That chain absorbs blue light, so it looks yellow.

    Markers 1 and 2
  2. 3.2

    Plant Colors

    Other colors from whole foods include beet red, paprika and beta-carotene.

    In your body
  3. 3.3

    Red 40

    Allura Red, also called Red 40 or E129, is a synthetic dye made from petroleum. An N=N azo bond joins two ring systems, and charged sulfonate groups make it dissolve in water.

    Markers 1 and 2
  4. 3.4

    Warnings and Phase-Outs

    In the EU, foods with Red 40 or five other synthetic colors must carry a warning about activity and attention in children. In 2025 the FDA said it plans to phase out petroleum-based dyes.

    Note
  5. 3.5

    Titanium Dioxide

    Titanium dioxide, E171, is a white mineral pigment. The EU banned it as a food additive from 2022, because harm to DNA couldn't be ruled out. The US still allows it, up to one percent of a food's weight.

    The molecule, then the facts
  1. 4.1

    Sorbic and Benzoic Acid

    The most common preservatives against molds and yeasts are sorbic acid, E200, and benzoic acid, E210, often used as their salts, potassium sorbate and sodium benzoate. In acidic foods they slip into microbe cells and stop them growing.

    Left, then right, then marker 1
  2. 4.2

    From Berries

    Sorbic acid was first found in rowan berries, and benzoic acid occurs naturally in cranberries and cinnamon. With vitamin C, heat and light, benzoate can form traces of benzene, so many drinks were reformulated.

    In your body
  3. 4.3

    Nitrite

    Nitrite, a nitrogen with two oxygens, cures bacon, ham and salami. Sodium nitrite is E250. It stops the bacterium that causes botulism, and gives cured meat its pink color and flavor.

    Marker 1
  4. 4.4

    Nitrosamines

    At high heat, nitrite can form nitrosamines, which we'll meet in Processing. Most nitrate in the diet actually comes from vegetables, and meats labeled uncured are usually cured with celery powder.

    Note
  5. 4.5

    Sulfites

    Sulfur dioxide and sulfites, E220 to E228, preserve wine and dried fruit. They can trigger asthma in sensitive people, so US labels must declare them, and they destroy thiamin.

    The molecule, then the facts
  1. 5.1

    Vitamin C

    Vitamin C, E300, is the most familiar antioxidant additive. The two OH groups on the double bond give up electrons, so vitamin C is oxidized first and the food is protected from browning.

    Marker 1
  2. 5.2

    BHA and BHT

    BHA and BHT, E320 and E321, are synthetic antioxidants. An OH on a ring gives up a hydrogen to stop the chain reaction that turns fat rancid, and bulky groups keep what's left stable.

    Markers 1 and 2
  3. 5.3

    A Caution

    They're permitted in small amounts. The US National Toxicology Program lists BHA as reasonably anticipated to be a human carcinogen, based on high doses in animals.

    Note
  4. 5.4

    Phosphoric Acid

    Phosphoric acid, E338, gives colas their tang. Phosphate additives are absorbed more completely than phosphorus in whole foods, which matters in kidney disease. Vinegar, yogurt and apples supply other food acids.

    The molecule, then the facts
  1. 6.1

    Pectin

    Pectin, E440, is long chains of galacturonic acid: a sugar ring with an acid group. The chains trap water and, with sugar and acid, set jam. It's a soluble fiber from apples and citrus peel.

    Marker 1
  2. 6.2

    Gums

    Most other thickeners come from plants too: agar and carrageenan from red seaweed, locust bean gum from carob, guar gum from guar beans. Xanthan gum is made by bacteria.

    The table, row by row
  3. 6.3

    Two Ends

    Emulsifiers keep oil and water mixed. This monoglyceride has a water-loving head, glycerol with two free OH groups, and a fat-loving tail, one fatty acid instead of a fat's three.

    Markers 1 and 2
  4. 6.4

    Lecithin

    Mono- and diglycerides, E471, keep bread soft. Lecithin, E322, works the same way; egg yolk lecithin holds mayonnaise together.

    In your body
  5. 6.5

    Gut Bacteria

    Early research in mice suggests some emulsifiers may disturb gut bacteria; evidence in people is still limited.

    Note
  1. 7.1

    Baking Soda

    Baking soda is sodium bicarbonate, E500. With an acid it gives off carbon dioxide, which makes batter rise. Your pancreas releases bicarbonate too, to neutralize stomach acid.

    Marker 1
  2. 7.2

    Anti-Caking

    Silica, E551, and calcium silicate soak up moisture so salt and spice mixes don't clump.

    The box
  3. 7.3

    MSG

    Monosodium glutamate, E621, is the amino acid glutamic acid with one acid group charged. In water it splits into glutamate and a sodium ion. Glutamate tastes savory, or umami.

    Markers 1 and 2
  4. 7.4

    Same as in Tomatoes

    Your body handles glutamate from MSG the same way as glutamate from tomatoes, parmesan, mushrooms or kombu. Blinded studies haven't consistently reproduced the symptoms some people report, and MSG has about a third of the sodium of salt.

    In your body
  1. 8.1

    Aspartame

    Aspartame, E951, is two amino acids, aspartic acid and phenylalanine, with a methyl cap. It's about two hundred times sweeter than sugar.

    Markers 1 to 3
  2. 8.2

    PKU and Safety

    People with PKU can't break down phenylalanine, so labels warn them. In 2023 the WHO's cancer agency classed aspartame as possibly carcinogenic, its lowest level, while WHO's expert committee kept the acceptable daily intake at forty milligrams per kilogram.

    Note
  3. 8.3

    Sucralose

    Compare sucrose, table sugar, with sucralose, E955. Three OH groups are swapped for chlorine. The result is about six hundred times sweeter and mostly isn't absorbed.

    Left, then right
  4. 8.4

    Erythritol

    Erythritol is a sugar alcohol: a short chain with an OH on every carbon. It's about two-thirds as sweet as sugar with almost no calories. Other sugar alcohols cause gas in large amounts, and xylitol is poisonous to dogs.

    Marker 1, then the facts
  5. 8.5

    The ADI

    The acceptable daily intake is the amount per kilogram that can be eaten every day for life without appreciable risk, usually a hundredth of the highest harmless dose in animals.

    The box

    Worth Knowing

    A few extras. An E-number is an approval, not a warning. Uncured meat is still cured, with celery powder. And additives cluster in ultra-processed foods; whole foods need almost none. Try the quiz below.

Quiz

Tap a question to see the answer

1. What does an E-number tell you?

That the additive has passed an EU safety review; the hundreds digit roughly gives its job (Figure 1).

2. How are additives shown on a US label?

By name, such as “sodium benzoate,” not by E-number (Figure 2).

3. Where does most added citric acid come from?

Fermenting sugar with a mold, Aspergillus niger (Figure 2).

4. Which additive did the EU ban in 2022, and why?

Titanium dioxide, E171, because harm to DNA could not be ruled out (Figure 3).

5. What does the E200 range do?

Preserves food against microbes (Figure 4).

6. Why is sodium nitrite (E250) in cured meat?

It stops the botulism bacterium and gives the pink color and cured flavor (Figure 4).

7. Who needs to watch for sulfites?

People with sulfite-sensitive asthma; US labels must declare 10 ppm or more (Figure 4).

8. What do BHA and BHT do?

They stop fats from going rancid (Figure 5).

9. What is pectin made of, and where does it come from?

Chains of galacturonic acid, from apples and citrus peel (Figure 6).

10. How does an emulsifier keep oil and water mixed?

It has a water-loving head and a fat-loving tail (Figure 6).

11. What is E621, and which whole foods are rich in the same compound?

Monosodium glutamate; tomatoes, parmesan, mushrooms, soy sauce, kombu (Figure 7).

12. How does sucralose differ from sugar?

Three OH groups of sucrose are replaced by chlorine, making it about 600 times sweeter (Figure 8).
