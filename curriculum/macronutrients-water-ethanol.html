Module 1 · Water and Ethanol

The last two energy-related molecules of Module 1: water, which gives no calories but makes up most of your body, and ethanol, which gives calories but no nutrition. Built from the atoms up: why water is polar and sticky, how much of you is water and how the balance is kept, how yeast makes ethanol from sugar, and how your liver breaks it down.   1. 1.1

    Two Small Molecules

    This lesson finishes Module 1 with two small molecules that sit beside the big three. Water gives no calories at all, yet it's the nutrient you need most. Ethanol, the alcohol in drinks, gives calories, but your body has no use for it.

    Title, then the root box
  2. 1.2

    The Water Branch

    On the left is water. We'll start with the molecule itself, which is bent and polar, and see why that lets it dissolve salts and sugars. Then we'll look at how much of your body is water, and how your body keeps the amount steady.

    Left branch: Water and its four boxes
  3. 1.3

    The Ethanol Branch

    On the right is ethanol. Yeast makes it from sugar by fermentation. Its smaller cousin, methanol, is a poison. Then we'll follow ethanol into the liver, where it's broken down, and look at its effects. Each box links to the figure that explains it.

    Right branch: Ethanol and its four boxes
  4. 1.4

    Why Water Matters

    You can live for weeks without food, but only a few days without water. It's the main ingredient of your body and of most whole foods.

    First box: water
  5. 1.5

    Ethanol and Labels

    Ethanol gives seven calories per gram, nearly as much as fat. Water only shows up in ingredient lists. In the US, most beer, wine and spirits are labeled under separate rules, so you see alcohol by volume rather than a Nutrition Facts panel.

    Second and third boxes
  1. 2.1

    Two Hydrogens, One Oxygen

    Here's water: one oxygen in the middle, bonded to two hydrogens. Oxygen makes two bonds and hydrogen makes one, the same rules as in every lesson so far. Every atom is shown.

    The molecule, marker 1
  2. 2.2

    Bent and Polar

    Oxygen pulls the shared electrons toward itself, so it ends up slightly negative and each hydrogen slightly positive. And the two bonds meet at about a hundred and four and a half degrees, so the molecule is bent like a V, not straight. One end negative, the other positive: chemists call that polar.

    Markers 1 and 2
  3. 2.3

    Hydrogen Bonds

    Because of that, water molecules cling to each other. The slightly positive hydrogen of one is drawn to the slightly negative oxygen of the next. That's a hydrogen bond, shown as the dashed lines. Each one is weak and lasts only a split second, but there are so many that water stays liquid at body temperature and takes a lot of heat to warm up.

    The cartoon, dashed lines
  4. 2.4

    Salts and Sugars Dissolve

    Polar water pulls table salt apart into sodium and chloride ions and wraps each one in water molecules. That's how minerals travel around your body. Sugars and many vitamins are covered in OH groups, which form hydrogen bonds with water, so they dissolve easily too.

    First two boxes
  5. 2.5

    Fats Don’t

    Fat chains are just carbon and hydrogen, with no charge for water to grab. So oil and water separate. That's why fats need bile to be digested and protein carriers to travel in your blood, as we saw in the Fats lesson.

    Third box
  1. 3.1

    How Much of You

    Water makes up about sixty percent of an adult man's body weight and around fifty to fifty-five percent of a woman's, because women on average carry more body fat, which holds little water. A newborn is about three quarters water. Muscle is about three quarters water too.

    The table, row by row
  2. 3.2

    Where It Is

    About two thirds of your body water is inside your cells. Most of the rest bathes the cells, and a smaller share is plasma, the liquid part of blood, which is over ninety percent water. Water moves freely between these spaces, following salts like sodium and potassium; that's one reason those minerals matter so much.

    The three boxes
  3. 3.3

    Carrier and Coolant

    Water is the medium everything travels in: blood carries nutrients, oxygen, hormones and waste, and urine carries waste out. It also keeps your temperature steady. When sweat evaporates, it takes heat with it.

    First two boxes
  4. 3.4

    Part of the Chemistry

    Water also takes part in chemistry. Every time you digest a protein or a starch, a water molecule is added back into each bond that's cut. That's hydrolysis. And water cushions your brain and spinal cord, lubricates your joints, and keeps your eyes and mouth moist.

    Third box
  1. 4.1

    In and Out

    Most of your water comes from drinks, and all drinks count, including milk, tea and coffee. About a fifth comes from food, and your cells make roughly a cup a day as they burn fuel. Water leaves as urine, sweat, the moisture in every breath, and a little in stool. Heat, exercise, fever, vomiting and diarrhea all raise the losses.

    The three boxes
  2. 4.2

    Keeping It Level

    When water runs low, your blood becomes slightly more concentrated. Your brain senses that and makes you thirsty, and the pituitary gland releases vasopressin, also called antidiuretic hormone. It tells the kidneys to save water, so urine becomes smaller in volume and darker. When you drink, the signals switch off.

    The five steps
  3. 4.3

    Water in Food

    Fruit and vegetables are mostly water. Cucumber, lettuce and celery are about ninety-five percent. Watermelon, strawberries and tomatoes are close behind. Even cooked oats and rice are mostly water, while bread is about a third and nuts very little.

    The table
  4. 4.4

    How Much You Need

    The US Institute of Medicine set an adequate intake for total water, from drinks and food together, of about two point seven liters a day for women and three point seven for men. About eighty percent of that usually comes from drinks. You need more in heat, with exercise, and when pregnant or breastfeeding.

    First box
  5. 4.5

    Too Little, Too Much

    Too little causes dehydration: thirst, dark urine, headache, tiredness and dizziness. Babies and older adults are most at risk, partly because older people feel thirst less. Too much, very fast, can dilute the sodium in your blood, called hyponatremia. That's rare, and mostly seen in long endurance events.

    Second and third boxes
  1. 5.1

    From Ethane

    Start with ethane: two carbons, each holding three hydrogens. Swap one of those hydrogens for an OH group, and you have ethanol. Any carbon chain carrying an OH like this is called an alcohol; ethanol is the one in drinks.

    Ethane, then ethanol, marker 1
  2. 5.2

    Why It Spreads So Fast

    The OH lets ethanol mix with water, and the small carbon end lets it slip through fatty cell membranes. So it spreads quickly into every tissue, including the brain. It gives seven calories per gram but has no nutritional role.

    In your body
  3. 5.3

    Fermentation

    Ethanol is made by yeast. Without oxygen, yeast splits each glucose into two ethanols and two carbon dioxides. Count the atoms: six carbons, twelve hydrogens and six oxygens on each side. Nothing is lost.

    Glucose, arrow, ethanol and carbon dioxide
  4. 5.4

    Bread and Drinks

    In bread, the carbon dioxide makes the dough rise and most of the ethanol bakes off. In wine and beer, the ethanol stays. Very ripe fruit, fruit juice and fermented foods like kefir and kombucha contain small traces.

    Note
  5. 5.5

    Methanol

    Methanol is the simplest alcohol: the same OH on just one carbon. It's far more dangerous. Your liver turns it into formaldehyde and formic acid, which damage the optic nerve, so even small amounts of pure methanol can cause blindness or death. It's a danger in illegally distilled spirits. The tiny amounts in fruit juice are harmless.

    Methanol, then the facts
  1. 6.1

    From Glass to Liver

    Some ethanol is absorbed in the stomach, and most in the small intestine, quickly. Food in the stomach slows it down. It then spreads through all your body water, which is why the same drink gives a smaller person a higher blood level. The liver clears roughly one standard drink an hour, and nothing speeds that up.

    The five steps
  2. 6.2

    Step One

    In the liver, an enzyme called alcohol dehydrogenase takes two hydrogens off ethanol. Look at the second carbon: it loses a hydrogen and the oxygen loses its hydrogen, leaving a C double bond O. That's acetaldehyde, a toxic, reactive molecule that causes much of alcohol's damage.

    Ethanol, marker 1, then acetaldehyde, marker 2
  3. 6.3

    Step Two

    A second enzyme, aldehyde dehydrogenase, adds an oxygen, making acetate, the same molecule that gives vinegar its sour taste. Cells burn acetate for energy or turn it into fat. Many people of East Asian descent carry a slow version of this second enzyme, so acetaldehyde builds up and their face flushes after even a small drink.

    Acetic acid, marker 3, then the note
  4. 6.4

    One Standard Drink

    In the US, one standard drink has fourteen grams of pure ethanol. That's about twelve fluid ounces of beer at five percent, five ounces of wine at twelve percent, or one and a half ounces of spirits at forty percent.

    The table
  5. 6.5

    Effects

    In the short term, alcohol slows reactions and judgment, makes you urinate more by blocking vasopressin, and disturbs sleep. A lot at once can cause alcohol poisoning, which can be fatal. Heavy use over years damages the liver and heart, can lead to dependence, and often leaves people short of thiamine and folate. The World Health Organization's cancer agency classifies alcoholic drinks as a cause of cancer, and no amount is known to be safe in pregnancy. For health, less is better. This is general education, not medical advice.

    The three boxes

  2. 7.2

    Worth Knowing

    A few extras. Coffee and tea still count toward your water. Ice floats because hydrogen bonds hold it in an open lattice. Cooking doesn't remove all the alcohol from a dish; a long simmer removes most, but rarely all. And in the US, a non-alcoholic drink can still contain up to half a percent alcohol. That's Module 1. Quiz

Tap a question to see the answer

1. How many kcal does a gram of ethanol give, and a gram of water?

About 7 for ethanol; 0 for water (Figure 1).

2. Why is a water molecule polar?

Oxygen pulls the shared electrons toward itself, leaving the O slightly negative and the two H slightly positive, and the molecule is bent (Figure 2).

3. What is a hydrogen bond?

A weak attraction between the slightly positive H of one molecule and the slightly negative O of another (Figure 2).

4. Why don’t oil and water mix?

Fat chains have no charges or OH groups for water to grab, so they separate (Figure 2).

5. Roughly how much of an adult’s body weight is water?

About 50 to 60%; more in babies (Figure 3).

6. Where is most of your body water?

Inside cells, about two thirds of it (Figure 3).

7. What hormone tells the kidneys to save water?

Vasopressin, also called antidiuretic hormone (Figure 4).

8. What is the US adequate intake for total water?

About 2.7 L a day for women and 3.7 L for men, from drinks and food together (Figure 4).

9. What does yeast make from one glucose during fermentation?

Two ethanols and two carbon dioxides (Figure 5).

10. Why is methanol dangerous?

The liver turns it into formaldehyde and formic acid, which can cause blindness or death (Figure 5).

11. What are the two steps that break down ethanol in the liver?

Ethanol to acetaldehyde (alcohol dehydrogenase), then acetaldehyde to acetate (aldehyde dehydrogenase) (Figure 6).

12. How much pure ethanol is in a US standard drink?

14 g: about 12 fl oz of beer, 5 fl oz of wine or 1.5 fl oz of spirits (Figure 6).
