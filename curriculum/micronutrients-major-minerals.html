Module 2 · Major Minerals

The third micronutrient lesson covers the seven major minerals: sodium, chloride, potassium, calcium, phosphorus, magnesium and sulfur, which you need in amounts over 100 mg a day. It starts with how atoms become ions, then shows each mineral as an ion or simple compound in ball-and-stick form, with what it does in your body, what happens with too little or too much, and where to find it in whole foods. The figure is on the left and the lesson script on the right; the quiz is at the end.

  1. 1.1

    Minerals

    Minerals are single elements your body needs but can't make. This lesson covers the seven major minerals, the ones you need in amounts over about a hundred milligrams a day.

    Title, then the root box
  2. 1.2

    Electrolytes

    On the left are the electrolytes: sodium, chloride and potassium. They're charged particles dissolved in your body fluids, and they control fluid balance and carry nerve signals.

    The left branch
  3. 1.3

    Bone Minerals and Sulfur

    In the middle are the bone minerals: calcium, phosphorus and magnesium. On the right is sulfur, which comes to you mainly in protein. Each box links to its figure.

    The middle and right branches
  4. 1.4

    Major Is About Amount

    Major means you need more of them, not that they matter more. Trace minerals like iron and zinc are needed in smaller amounts but are just as essential.

    First box
  5. 1.5

    Elements and Ions

    Because a mineral is an element, it can't be destroyed by cooking or made by the body. And in food and in your body, minerals almost always exist as charged atoms called ions.

    The last two boxes
  1. 2.1

    Giving and Taking

    A sodium atom easily gives away one electron. With one more proton than electrons, it becomes a positive sodium ion. A chlorine atom takes one electron and becomes a negative chloride ion.

    Sodium ion, then chloride ion
  2. 2.2

    Ionic Bonds

    Opposite charges attract. That attraction, an ionic bond, holds salt together. Notice chloride is bigger than sodium; it really is.

    Note
  3. 2.3

    The Salt Crystal

    In a salt crystal, sodium and chloride ions alternate. Every sodium is surrounded by chlorides, and every chloride by sodiums. Here the sticks show neighbors, not shared electrons.

    Markers 1 and 2
  4. 2.4

    Different Sizes

    Here are the other ions at the same scale. Magnesium and calcium give away two electrons each, so they carry a charge of two plus. Potassium is noticeably bigger than sodium, and cells use that difference to tell them apart.

    Left to right
  5. 2.5

    The Seven

    This table lists the main form of each major mineral in your body, and its charge. Phosphorus and sulfur travel as phosphate and sulfate, small groups of atoms with oxygen.

    The table, row by row
  1. 3.1

    Salt Dissolves

    When salt meets water, water molecules crowd around each ion. A sodium ion attracts the slightly negative oxygen ends of water; a chloride ion attracts the slightly positive hydrogen ends. Surrounded like this, the ions drift apart, and salt dissolves.

    Markers 1 to 3
  2. 3.2

    Water Follows Sodium

    Sodium is the main positive ion in the fluid outside your cells. Where sodium goes, water follows, so sodium sets your fluid volume and helps control blood pressure.

    In your body
  3. 3.3

    Sodium Facts

    You need about fifteen hundred milligrams a day. Running short from diet is rare. The common problem is too much: in many people it raises blood pressure, which increases the risk of heart disease and stroke. US guidance is to keep under twenty-three hundred milligrams; most Americans eat about thirty-four hundred.

    The facts
  4. 3.4

    Stomach Acid

    Chloride is the main negative ion outside cells, paired with sodium. Your stomach also uses it to make hydrochloric acid, which splits in water into a hydrogen ion, making the stomach acidic, and chloride.

    Marker 1
  5. 3.5

    Chloride Facts

    Chloride comes mostly with salt, so the concern with too much is really the sodium. Seaweed, tomatoes, celery and olives also supply it.

    In your body
  1. 4.1

    Potassium Chloride

    Potassium chloride forms the same grid as table salt, with bigger potassium ions in place of sodium. It's the main ingredient of salt substitutes.

    Marker 1
  2. 4.2

    Inside and Outside

    About ninety-eight percent of your potassium is inside your cells, while most sodium stays outside.

    The first two steps
  3. 4.3

    The Pump

    A protein in every cell membrane, the sodium-potassium pump, keeps it that way, pushing three sodium ions out and bringing two potassium ions in, using energy from ATP. The difference in charge lets nerves fire, muscles contract and your heart beat steadily.

    The last two steps
  4. 4.4

    Potassium Facts

    Adults need about twenty-six hundred to thirty-four hundred milligrams a day, and many people eat less, which is linked with higher blood pressure. Beans, potatoes, squash, leafy greens, bananas and yogurt are rich sources.

    The facts
  5. 4.5

    A Caution

    Healthy kidneys remove extra potassium from food. With kidney disease or some blood-pressure medicines it can build up and disturb the heart rhythm, so salt substitutes and supplements need a doctor's advice.

    Note
  1. 5.1

    Calcium Carbonate

    A calcium ion carries two plus charges. Here it's paired with carbonate, a carbon with three oxygens and two minus charges. Calcium carbonate is chalk, eggshell and seashell, and the most common calcium supplement.

    Markers 1 and 2
  2. 5.2

    Where It Is

    About ninety-nine percent of your calcium is in bones and teeth, as hard crystals with phosphate. The other one percent runs muscle contraction, nerve signals and blood clotting.

    The first two boxes
  3. 5.3

    Bone Is a Bank

    Your body keeps blood calcium in a narrow range. When you eat too little, hormones and vitamin D draw it from bone, so low intake shows up years later as weak bones.

    The third box
  4. 5.4

    Calcium Facts

    Adults need a thousand to twelve hundred milligrams a day. Too little over time leads to osteoporosis; too much, usually from supplements, can cause kidney stones. Dairy, sardines and canned salmon with bones, calcium-set tofu, kale and bok choy are good sources.

    The facts
  5. 5.5

    Spinach

    Spinach has plenty of calcium, but a compound called oxalate binds most of it, so little is absorbed. We'll meet oxalate in the antinutrients lesson.

    Note
  1. 6.1

    Phosphate

    In your body, phosphorus is almost always phosphate: one phosphorus atom with four oxygens. At the pH of blood, much of it carries one hydrogen.

    Markers 1 and 2
  2. 6.2

    Phosphorus Facts

    About eighty-five percent is in bone, with calcium. Phosphate is also part of DNA, of cell membranes and of ATP. Adults need seven hundred milligrams a day, easily supplied by dairy, meat, fish, beans and nuts. Shortage is rare; build-up is a problem in kidney disease.

    In your body
  3. 6.3

    Magnesium in Water

    Magnesium is small and doubly charged, so it holds six water molecules tightly around it, much more tightly than sodium does.

    Markers 1 and 2
  4. 6.4

    Magnesium Facts

    About half your magnesium is in bone. It's needed by over three hundred enzymes, and ATP works mostly paired with magnesium. Magnesium sits at the center of chlorophyll, which is why green vegetables are a good source, along with seeds, nuts, beans and whole grains.

    In your body
  5. 6.5

    Too Little or Too Much

    Many people eat less magnesium than recommended, though true deficiency is uncommon. Food can't give you too much, but supplements over three hundred and fifty milligrams a day can cause diarrhea.

    Note
  1. 7.1

    Sulfur Amino Acids

    Most of your sulfur comes in two amino acids. Methionine, which is essential, has sulfur in the middle of its side chain. Cysteine has an SH group at the end.

    Markers 1 and 2
  2. 7.2

    Disulfide Bridges

    Two cysteines in a protein can join through their sulfurs, forming a bridge that holds proteins like keratin, in hair and nails, in shape.

    Note
  3. 7.3

    Sulfate

    Extra sulfur from amino acids becomes sulfate, one sulfur with four oxygens. It's used to build cartilage and to help the liver clear some drugs; the rest leaves in urine.

    Marker 3
  4. 7.4

    Sulfur Facts

    Sulfur is the third most plentiful mineral in your body. It's part of glutathione, an important antioxidant, and of thiamin and biotin. There's no separate requirement: enough protein supplies enough sulfur. Garlic, onions and cabbage-family vegetables also contain sulfur compounds that give them their smell.

    In your body
  1. 8.1

    On the Label

    Sodium must appear on every Nutrition Facts label. Since the 2016 update, potassium and calcium must too. The Daily Values are listed in the table.

    The table, row by row
  2. 8.2

    Salt Versus Sodium

    Salt is about forty percent sodium. One teaspoon of salt has about twenty-three hundred milligrams of sodium, a whole day's Daily Value. Sea salt, kosher salt and pink salt are all mostly sodium chloride.

    The first two boxes
  3. 8.3

    Where It Comes From

    Most sodium in US diets comes from packaged and restaurant foods, not the salt shaker, so cooking from whole foods makes it easier to control.

    The third box
  4. 8.4

    Five and Twenty

    A quick guide: five percent of the Daily Value or less is low, twenty percent or more is high. Aim low for sodium, high for potassium and calcium.

    The two boxes

    Worth Knowing

Quiz

Tap a question to see the answer

1. What makes a mineral “major” rather than “trace”?

You need it in amounts over about 100 mg a day; it is not about importance (Figure 1).

2. Why is a sodium ion positive?

It has given away one electron, so it has one more proton than electrons (Figure 2).

3. Which is larger, a sodium ion or a chloride ion?

Chloride (Figure 2).

4. Which end of a water molecule faces a sodium ion?

The slightly negative oxygen end (Figure 3).

5. What acid does your stomach make from chloride?

Hydrochloric acid, HCl (Figure 3).

6. Where is most of your potassium, inside or outside cells?

Inside, about 98% (Figure 4).

7. What does the sodium-potassium pump do?

Pushes 3 sodium ions out and brings 2 potassium ions in, using ATP (Figure 4).

8. What share of your calcium is in bones and teeth?

About 99% (Figure 5).

9. Name three things phosphate is part of besides bone.

DNA, phospholipids in membranes, ATP (Figure 6).

10. Why are green vegetables a good source of magnesium?

Magnesium is at the center of chlorophyll (Figure 6).

11. Which two amino acids contain sulfur?

Methionine and cysteine (Figure 7).

12. About how much sodium is in a teaspoon of salt?

About 2,300 mg, a full day’s Daily Value (Figure 8).
