Module 3 · Processing

Processing is everything that happens to food between the farm and your plate. This lesson follows processing aids that never reach the label; the compounds that form when food is browned, grilled, smoked or cured (acrylamide, AGEs, HCAs, PAHs and nitrosamines); and methods that change the food itself: fermenting, pasteurizing, hydrogenating, refining and nixtamalizing. Each molecule is shown in ball-and-stick form with what it does, the risks, and where it is found. The figure is on the left and the lesson script on the right; the quiz is at the end.

  1. 1.1

    Processing

    This lesson is about processing: everything that happens to food between the farm and your plate. Almost every food is processed somehow, and this tree sorts what we'll cover into three groups.

    Title, then the root box
  2. 1.2

    Unlisted Helpers

    On the left are processing aids, like the solvent hexane, which help make a food and then are removed, so they never reach the label.

    The left branch
  3. 1.3

    Made by Heat

    In the middle are compounds that form when food is heated hard. Browning makes acrylamide and AGEs, grilling and smoking make HCAs and PAHs, and curing plus frying makes nitrosamines.

    The middle branch
  4. 1.4

    Changing the Food

    On the right are methods that change the food itself: fermenting, pasteurizing, hydrogenating, refining and nixtamalizing. Each box links to its figure.

    The right branch
  5. 1.5

    Good, Bad and Both

    Processing isn't good or bad as a whole. Pasteurizing and fermenting make food safer, nixtamalizing makes corn more nutritious, charring and partial hydrogenation make things worth limiting, and refining trades nutrients for shelf life.

    The three boxes
  1. 2.1

    Hexane

    Here's hexane: six carbons in a row with nothing but hydrogens around them. With no oxygen, it mixes with oil, not water.

    Marker 1
  2. 2.2

    Pulling Out Oil

    That's why it's used to make most soybean, canola, corn and sunflower oil. Crushed seeds are washed with hexane, which dissolves the oil out, and then the hexane is boiled off and recycled.

  3. 2.3

    How Much Is Left

    Hexane isn't a nutrient. Breathing high levels at work can damage nerves, but the traces left in oil are tiny; the EU caps them at one milligram per kilogram. Expeller-pressed, cold-pressed and USDA organic oils are made without it.

  4. 2.4

    Other Aids

    Other processing aids include enzymes that clear fruit juice, fining agents like gelatin or clay that pull haze out of wine and beer, anti-foaming agents, and carryover additives that came in with an ingredient.

    The table, row by row
  5. 2.5

    Why Not Listed

    US and EU rules let manufacturers leave these off the label when they're removed or do nothing in the final food. The exception is allergens: if an aid comes from fish, milk or another major allergen, it must still be declared.

    The two boxes
  1. 3.1

    Asparagine

    Browning starts with amino acids. Asparagine is plentiful in potatoes and grains. Heated above about 120 degrees Celsius with a sugar like glucose, it loses its amino-acid end.

    Markers 1 and 2
  2. 3.2

    Acrylamide

    What's left is acrylamide: a reactive double bond next to the same amide group. It's part of the Maillard reaction, the browning that colors crusts, chips and coffee, and it was found in food in 2002.

    Markers 3 and 4
  3. 3.3

    The Risk

    The body turns acrylamide into a compound that can damage DNA. It causes cancer in animals at high doses, and IARC calls it probably carcinogenic to humans. Studies in people haven't shown a clear link, but the advice is to fry, bake and toast to golden, not dark brown.

    In your body
  4. 3.4

    Found In

    It's in fries, chips, crackers, cookies, toast, cereals and coffee. Boiled and steamed foods have essentially none.

  5. 3.5

    AGEs

    Sugars can also stick to proteins. Lysine's side chain ends in a free NH2 group, and after several steps it can carry this small acid group. That's carboxymethyl-lysine, the best-known AGE, or advanced glycation end product.

    Markers 5 and 6
  6. 3.6

    AGEs in Food

    AGEs build up in the body with age and in diabetes, and food adds some, mostly from meat grilled, roasted or fried. How much food AGEs matter is still debated. Stewing, poaching and steaming make far fewer.

  1. 4.1

    HCAs

    This is PhIP, a heterocyclic amine, or HCA: rings containing nitrogen with an amino group. HCAs form when creatine, amino acids and sugar in muscle meat react at very high heat.

    Markers 1 and 2
  2. 4.2

    HCA Risk

    The liver activates HCAs into forms that damage DNA, and IARC lists PhIP as possibly carcinogenic. The National Cancer Institute suggests avoiding direct flame, turning meat often, and cutting off charred parts.

    In your body
  3. 4.3

    PAHs

    Benzo a pyrene is a PAH: five benzene rings fused into one flat sheet. PAHs form when fat drips onto flames and the smoke coats the food.

    Marker 3
  4. 4.4

    PAH Risk

    IARC classifies benzo a pyrene as carcinogenic to humans, and the EU limits it in smoked foods. Trim fat, avoid flare-ups, and scrape off char. Cigarette smoke is a far bigger source than food.

    In your body
  5. 4.5

    Smoke Flavor

    Guaiacol is a benzene ring with an OH and a methoxy group side by side. It forms when wood burns and gives smoked foods their aroma, and phenols like it slow spoilage.

    Markers 4 and 5
  6. 4.6

    Smoked Foods

    Guaiacol itself isn't a concern; the concern with smoking is the PAHs that come with the smoke. Liquid smoke is filtered, so most PAHs are removed.

  1. 5.1

    Nitrite

    Nitrite is the curing salt from the Additives lesson. It keeps cured meat pink, adds flavor and stops the bacterium that causes botulism.

    Marker 1
  2. 5.2

    Making a Nitrosamine

    Amines like dimethylamine come from protein breakdown. In frying heat or stomach acid, nitrite puts an N=O group on the amine's nitrogen, making a nitrosamine. NDMA is the most common one.

    Markers 2 and 3
  3. 5.3

    Nitrosamine Risk

    Nitrosamines cause cancer in many animal species, and IARC calls NDMA probably carcinogenic. They're thought to be one reason processed meat is classed as carcinogenic to humans, mainly for colorectal cancer. Crisp-fried bacon forms the most.

    In your body
  4. 5.4

    Vitamin C Blocks It

    Vitamin C, as ascorbate or erythorbate, reacts with nitrite first and blocks the reaction. US rules for bacon require it alongside the nitrite.

    First box
  5. 5.5

    Uncured and Vegetables

    Meat labeled uncured is usually cured with celery powder, which bacteria turn into nitrite, so the chemistry is the same. Vegetables are different: they supply most dietary nitrate, along with vitamin C, and are linked to lower cancer risk.

    The last two boxes
  1. 6.1

    Lactic Acid

    Fermentation uses microbes to change food. Lactic acid bacteria turn sugar into lactic acid, which sours milk into yogurt and preserves cabbage as sauerkraut and kimchi.

    Marker 1
  2. 6.2

    Alcohol, Gas and Vinegar

    Yeast turns sugar into ethanol and carbon dioxide; in bread the gas raises the dough and the alcohol bakes off. Then Acetobacter bacteria can turn ethanol into acetic acid: vinegar.

    Markers 2 to 4
  3. 6.3

    Why Ferment

    The acid keeps harmful bacteria from growing, and fermentation can predigest food: yogurt has less lactose, and sourdough less phytate. The things to watch are alcohol and the salt in some ferments.

    In your body
  4. 6.4

    Pasteurization

    Pasteurization uses just enough heat to kill germs. Most milk is heated to 72 degrees Celsius for 15 seconds; ultra-high temperature milk gets about 135 to 150 degrees for a few seconds and keeps without refrigeration until opened.

    The table, row by row
  5. 6.5

    Why Pasteurize

    It kills Listeria, Salmonella, E. coli and Campylobacter while barely changing the nutrients. US health agencies warn that raw milk is riskier, especially for children, pregnant women and older adults.

    The two boxes
  1. 7.1

    Adding Hydrogen

    Hydrogenation bubbles hydrogen gas through hot oil with a nickel catalyst. A four-carbon piece stands in for a fatty acid here, with a natural cis double bond.

    The first cell, then marker 1
  2. 7.2

    Full or Partial

    Add both hydrogens and the double bond becomes single: a straight, solid, saturated chain. Stop partway and some double bonds flip to trans instead. That's trans fat, from Figure 7 of the Fats lesson.

    Markers 2 and 3
  3. 7.3

    Trans Fat Rules

    Industrial trans fat raises LDL and lowers HDL. In 2015 the FDA ruled partially hydrogenated oils no longer generally recognized as safe, and they've been removed from US foods. Fully hydrogenated oil is saturated fat with almost no trans.

  4. 7.4

    Refining Grain

    Refining grain removes the bran and germ, leaving the starchy endosperm. That loses fiber, B vitamins, iron, magnesium, zinc and vitamin E. Enriched flour gets some B vitamins, folic acid and iron back, but not the fiber or the other minerals.

    The table, column by column
  5. 7.5

    Refining Oil

    Most oils are also refined, bleached and deodorized. That makes them clear, mild and stable for frying, but strips much of their vitamin E and polyphenols. Extra-virgin olive oil and cold-pressed oils skip these steps.

  1. 8.1

    Lime

    Nixtamalization starts with slaked lime, calcium hydroxide. In water it breaks into calcium ions and hydroxide, making a strongly alkaline soak.

    The first two cells
  2. 8.2

    Freeing Niacin

    In raw corn, niacin, vitamin B3, is tied to other molecules and can't be absorbed. The alkaline soak cuts it free.

    Markers 2 and 3
  3. 8.3

    Other Gains

    It also loosens the hull, adds calcium, improves the balance of available amino acids, lowers some mold toxins, and lets the dough hold together for tortillas.

    In your body
  4. 8.4

    Pellagra

    Where corn became a staple without it, in parts of Europe and the US South, pellagra was common: niacin deficiency, with dermatitis, diarrhea and dementia.

  5. 8.5

    Corn to Tortilla

    The steps are simple: cook dried corn with lime, steep it overnight, rinse, and grind into masa. Corn tortillas, tamales and hominy are nixtamalized; cornmeal, grits and polenta usually aren't.

    The steps, left to right
  1. 9.1

    Where Processed Foods Act

    Here's where compounds from cooking and processing act: first inside a single cell, then everywhere else in the body. Some are made by heat, some by curing, and some by refining.

    The cell drawing and the list of places
  2. 9.2

    Inside a Cell

    Processing changes what arrives at your cells. High-heat browning makes compounds like acrylamide and AGEs, and charring meat makes heterocyclic amines. The liver's smooth ER processes them, sometimes turning them into forms that can damage DNA. Refining strips some vitamins the mitochondria rely on, which is why many flours are fortified.

    In food and in the body

    Acrylamide. Forms when starchy foods brown at high heat (fries, toast, chips).

    AGEs. Form with browning, grilling and roasting; the body also makes them when blood sugar is high.

    Fortification. Refined flour is often enriched with iron, B vitamins and folic acid.

    The cell drawing and its numbered parts
  3. 9.3

    Digestion

    Cooking softens starch and protein so the small intestine digests them faster, and nixtamalization frees niacin in corn and adds calcium from the lime. In the stomach, nitrite from cured meat can form nitrosamines.

    In food and in the body

    Nitrosamines. Formed from nitrite and amines in cured meats, especially when fried, and in the stomach. Some cause cancer in animals.

    In the list: stomach, small intestine and bones
  4. 9.4

    Liver and Blood Vessels

    The liver activates and clears acrylamide, HCAs and PAHs, and some of the products can damage DNA, which is why charring and very browned starchy food are best kept moderate. Trans fat from partial hydrogenation raises LDL cholesterol and harms blood vessels. The kidneys clear what's left in urine.

    In food and in the body

    Acrylamide. Formed when starchy foods such as fries, chips and toast brown at high heat. The liver turns it into glycidamide, which can damage DNA.

    HCAs and PAHs. HCAs form in meat cooked very hot, PAHs in smoke and charring. The liver activates and clears both.

    In the list: liver, blood vessels and kidneys
  5. 9.5

    Fermentation and the Gut

    Live cultures from yogurt and other fermented foods meet the gut bacteria in the large intestine. Eating a lot of processed meat is linked to colon cancer.

    In food and in the body

    Live cultures. Bacteria such as Lactobacillus in yogurt and kefir. Many die in the stomach, and those that survive pass through the large intestine; most don’t stay there for long.

    Processed meat. Bacon, ham and sausages, preserved with salt and nitrite. Eating a lot of it is linked to colon cancer.

    In the list: the large intestine
  6. 9.6

    Simplified

    This is simplified. The amounts in normal diets are small, and the links to disease come from large studies of people, so the list shows where these compounds act, not a measure of harm.

Quiz

Tap a question to see the answer

1. What is a processing aid, and why is it usually not on the label?

A substance used during manufacturing that is removed or has no function in the finished food; US and EU rules exempt it, unless it comes from a major allergen (Figure 2).

2. What is hexane used for?

Dissolving oil out of crushed seeds such as soybeans and canola; it is then evaporated off (Figure 2).

3. Which two things react to form acrylamide?

The amino acid asparagine and a sugar, heated above about 120°C (Figure 3).

4. How can you form less acrylamide when making fries or toast?

Cook to golden yellow rather than dark brown (Figure 3).

5. What is an AGE?

A protein or fat permanently altered by a sugar, such as carboxymethyl-lysine; they form in browned food and in the body (Figure 3).

6. Where do HCAs and PAHs come from?

HCAs form in muscle meat cooked very hot; PAHs form when fat drips on flames and the smoke coats the food (Figure 4).

7. What gives smoked foods their smoky aroma?

Phenols such as guaiacol, made when wood lignin burns (Figure 4).

8. What does nitrite react with to form a nitrosamine?

An amine from protein breakdown, especially in frying heat or stomach acid (Figure 5).

9. Why is ascorbate or erythorbate added to cured bacon?

It reacts with nitrite first and blocks nitrosamine formation (Figure 5).

10. What do lactic acid bacteria and yeast each make from sugar?

Lactic acid bacteria make lactic acid; yeast makes ethanol and carbon dioxide (Figure 6).

11. What does partial hydrogenation create that full hydrogenation does not?

Trans fat, when some double bonds flip from cis to trans instead of being filled (Figure 7).

12. Why did pellagra appear where corn was eaten without nixtamalization?

Corn’s niacin is bound and cannot be absorbed unless the corn is cooked with lime (Figure 8).
