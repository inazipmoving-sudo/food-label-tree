Module 3 · Antinutrients

Antinutrients are natural compounds in plant foods that reduce how well you absorb or use a nutrient: phytate, oxalate, tannins, lectins, trypsin inhibitors and goitrogens. Each is shown in ball-and-stick form or as a labeled cartoon, with what it does, who should pay attention, where it is found, and how soaking, sprouting, fermenting and cooking reduce it.   1. 1.1

    Antinutrients

    This lesson is about antinutrients: compounds that plants make naturally which reduce how well you absorb or use a nutrient. There are six main ones, and this tree sorts them by what they block.

    Title, then the root box
  2. 1.2

    Three Groups

    Phytate, oxalate and tannins bind minerals like iron, zinc and calcium. Lectins and trypsin inhibitors get in the way of protein digestion. And goitrogens get in the way of iodine.

    The second and third rows, left to right
  3. 1.3

    Foods and Fixes

    Under each one are the foods it's highest in, and the kitchen fix: soaking, sprouting, fermenting, boiling or simply cooking. Each box links to its figure.

    The bottom row
  4. 1.4

    Why Plants Make Them

    Plants don't make these to harm us. They store phosphorus, or deter insects and animals. They just happen to interfere with a nutrient when we eat them.

    First box
  5. 1.5

    Not on the Label

    Traditional ways of preparing grains and beans grew up partly because they reduce antinutrients. And because they occur naturally, you'll never see them on a label.

    The last two boxes
  1. 2.1

    The Minerals

    Let's start with what gets blocked. Zinc, iron and calcium travel through your gut as small positive ions, drawn here at the same scale as every molecule on the page.

    The three ions
  2. 2.2

    Gripping Ions

    Many antinutrients carry negative oxygen groups that grip those ions. The clump is too big or too insoluble to cross the gut wall, so the mineral simply passes through.

    In your body
  3. 2.3

    Three Ways

    There are three ways to block a nutrient: grip minerals, block a digestive enzyme or stick to the gut wall, or get in the way of the thyroid.

    The table, row by row
  4. 2.4

    Dose and Context

    For most people eating a varied diet, antinutrients matter little. They matter more when one staple food supplies most of the calories, when mineral intake is already low, or in vegan diets that rely on grains and beans for iron and zinc.

    First box
  5. 2.5

    Often Useful Too

    And many of the same compounds have benefits. Phytate and tannins are antioxidants, and the foods richest in them are linked to better health. We'll meet that side in the Bioactives lesson.

    NoteSecond box
  1. 3.1

    Two Building Blocks

    Phytate is built from two parts. Inositol is a ring of six carbons with an OH on every one, a close cousin of glucose. And phosphate is the same group that's in DNA and ATP.

    Markers 1 and 2
  2. 3.2

    Six Phosphates

    Put a phosphate on all six carbons and you get phytic acid. In the gut its phosphates lose hydrogens and carry negative charges, and those grip zinc, iron and calcium.

    Markers 3 and 4
  3. 3.3

    The Seed’s Store

    Phytate is how seeds store phosphorus for the sprouting plant. Humans make very little phytase, the enzyme that cuts it apart, so most phytate passes through, taking bound minerals with it. It's the main thing in food that lowers zinc absorption.

    In your body
  4. 3.4

    Too Much

    Diets high in phytate and low in meat can lead to zinc and iron deficiency, especially where one unrefined grain or bean is the main food. But phytate is also an antioxidant, and whole-grain diets are linked to lower disease risk.

  5. 3.5

    Found In

    It's in the bran of whole grains, beans, lentils, soy, nuts and seeds. Refined white flour has much less, because the bran is removed.

  1. 4.1

    Oxalic Acid

    Oxalic acid is the smallest acid with two acid groups: just two carbons and four oxygens.

    Marker 1
  2. 4.2

    Grabbing Calcium

    In food it loses both hydrogens, leaving oxalate with two negative charges, and those clamp onto calcium. Calcium oxalate barely dissolves, so it forms crystals.

    Markers 2 and 3
  3. 4.3

    Spinach Calcium

    Oxalate binds calcium in the gut, so the calcium in spinach is absorbed only about a fifth as well as the calcium in milk.

    In your body
  4. 4.4

    Kidney Stones

    Absorbed oxalate leaves in urine, where it can join calcium to form kidney stones; most kidney stones are calcium oxalate. People who form stones are often told to limit high-oxalate foods, drink plenty of fluid, and eat calcium with those foods, which binds the oxalate in the gut.

  5. 4.5

    Found In

    Spinach, rhubarb, Swiss chard, beet greens, almonds, cashews, cocoa and sweet potatoes are high in oxalate. Rhubarb leaves are poisonous, so eat only the stalks.

  1. 5.1

    Gallic Acid

    Tannins are big polyphenols built from two kinds of building block. Gallic acid has three OH groups side by side on a ring, a strong grip for iron. Many gallic acids joined to a sugar make a hydrolyzable tannin.

    Markers 1 and 2
  2. 5.2

    Catechin

    Catechin has two neighboring OH groups on its ring, the same kind of grip. Chains of catechin-type units make condensed tannins.

    Markers 3 and 4
  3. 5.3

    Astringency

    Tannins bind proteins, and that's the dry, puckering feel of strong tea, red wine and unripe fruit.

  4. 5.4

    Iron

    They also bind nonheme iron. Tea with a meal can cut the iron you absorb from it by more than half, and coffee does something similar. Heme iron from meat is much less affected. So if your iron is low, drink tea and coffee between meals.

    In your body
  5. 5.5

    Found In

    Tea, red wine, coffee, cocoa, pomegranate, persimmons, grape skins, sorghum and colored beans are rich in tannins and related polyphenols.

  1. 6.1

    Lectins

    Lectins are proteins that bind specific sugars. The cells lining your gut are coated with sugar chains, and some lectins clamp onto them. They resist digestion and can damage the lining.

    The cartoon
  2. 6.2

    Raw Kidney Beans

    The best-known lectin is in red kidney beans. Raw or undercooked, as few as four or five beans can cause severe vomiting and diarrhea within hours. Beans warmed but not boiled, as in some slow cookers, can be worse than raw.

    In your body
  3. 6.3

    Boil Them

    The fix is simple: soak dried beans, pour off the water, and boil them hard. Boiling destroys almost all lectin activity, and canned beans are already cooked.

  4. 6.4

    Trypsin Inhibitors

    Trypsin is an enzyme from your pancreas that cuts proteins apart. A trypsin inhibitor is a small protein that fits tightly into trypsin's cutting site, like a plug, so less protein gets digested. Heat unfolds the inhibitor so it no longer fits.

    Stages 1 to 3
  5. 6.5

    Raw Soybeans

    Animals fed raw soybeans grow poorly. In people it's rarely a problem, because soy is almost always cooked, toasted or fermented. Raw egg white and raw potatoes have inhibitors too.

  1. 7.1

    Iodide

    Your thyroid pulls iodide from the blood to make thyroid hormone. Here's iodide, a large negative ion.

    The first ion
  2. 7.2

    Thiocyanate

    Thiocyanate is a small negative ion about the same size, so it competes with iodide for the pump that pulls iodide into the thyroid.

    Marker 1
  3. 7.3

    Goitrin

    Goitrin forms from a glucosinolate in cabbage-family vegetables when they're cut or chewed. Its C=S group interferes with the enzyme that attaches iodine to make the hormone.

    Markers 2 and 3
  4. 7.4

    Goiter

    With too little iodine and too much of these, the thyroid enlarges to compensate. That's a goiter, which is where the name goitrogen comes from.

    In your body
  5. 7.5

    Who Should Care

    It's a real problem mainly where iodine is low and a goitrogenic food is a staple, notably cassava. Normal servings of cooked broccoli, kale or cabbage aren't a concern if you get enough iodine.

  1. 8.1

    Soaking and Sprouting

    Soaking draws out water-soluble compounds and wakes up the seed's own phytase; pour the water away. Sprouting goes further: the seed breaks down its phytate store to feed the shoot.

    The first two rows
  2. 8.2

    Fermenting

    Fermenting lets microbes break compounds down. Sourdough bread, with its long fermentation, has much less phytate than ordinary yeast bread.

    Row 3
  3. 8.3

    Boiling and Milling

    Boiling unfolds proteins like lectins and trypsin inhibitors, and leaches soluble compounds into water you throw away. Milling and peeling remove antinutrients in the bran or skin, but take fiber, vitamins and minerals with them.

    The last two rows
  4. 8.4

    Pairing Foods

    Pairing helps too. Vitamin C with plant iron counteracts phytate and tannins. Calcium with high-oxalate foods binds the oxalate. And tea and coffee are best between meals.

    The three boxes
  5. 8.5

    Heat-Stable or Not

    Lectins and trypsin inhibitors are proteins, so thorough cooking destroys most of them. Phytate, oxalate and tannins are small, sturdy molecules that survive heat; they're reduced mainly by soaking, sprouting, fermenting, or boiling and draining.

    Note
  1. 9.1

    Where Antinutrients Act

    Here's where antinutrients act: first inside a single cell, then everywhere else in the body. Most work inside the gut, on the minerals and protein in the meal you eat them with. A few are absorbed and reach the kidneys or the thyroid.

    The cell drawing and the list of places
  2. 9.2

    Inside a Cell

    Antinutrients mostly work before nutrients reach your cells. Phytates and oxalates bind minerals in the gut, and trypsin inhibitors block protein digestion. A few reach the cell itself: raw bean lectins stick to the sugars on gut cells, and cyanide from unprocessed cassava blocks the mitochondria. Cooking, soaking and fermenting deal with most of them.

    In food and in the body

    Phytate. In whole grains, beans and nuts; soaking, sprouting and fermenting reduce it.

    Lectins. In raw beans and grains; proper cooking makes beans safe.

    Trypsin inhibitors. In raw soy and legumes; they block a protein-digesting enzyme in the gut and are destroyed by heat.

    The cell drawing and its numbered parts
  3. 9.3

    In the Gut

    In the small intestine, phytate, oxalate and tannins bind iron, zinc and calcium, so less is absorbed. Raw lectins resist digestion and can upset the gut, and trypsin inhibitors block the protein-digesting enzyme the pancreas sends in. Bacteria in the large intestine break down some of what's left.

    In food and in the body

    Phytate. In whole grains, beans, nuts and seeds. It binds iron and zinc in the gut; soaking, sprouting and fermenting break much of it down.

    Trypsin inhibitors. In raw soybeans and other beans. They block trypsin, a protein-digesting enzyme; cooking destroys most of them.

    In the list: small intestine, stomach, pancreas and large intestine
  4. 9.4

    Beyond the Gut

    A little oxalate is absorbed and leaves in urine, where it can join calcium to form kidney stones. Goitrogens from raw cabbage-family vegetables and cassava can reduce how much iodine the thyroid takes up, mainly when iodine is already low.

    In food and in the body

    Oxalate. In spinach, rhubarb, beet greens, nuts and tea. Most stays in the gut bound to calcium; the absorbed part leaves in urine.

    In the list: kidneys and thyroid
  5. 9.5

    Simplified

    This is simplified. In a varied diet, and after soaking or cooking, these effects are small, and many of these compounds have benefits too.

  2. 10.2

    Worth Knowing

    A few extras. The same compound can be an antinutrient or a helpful bioactive, depending on the amount, the rest of the diet and how the food is prepared. Cassava, bitter almonds and apricot kernels release cyanide unless they're properly prepared. And green potatoes make solanine, which cooking doesn't destroy, so cut away green parts and sprouts.

Quiz

Tap a question to see the answer

1. What is an antinutrient?

A natural compound in food that reduces how well you absorb or use a nutrient (Figure 1).

2. Why do phytate, oxalate and tannins hold on to minerals?

They carry negative oxygen groups that grip positive mineral ions such as Zn²⁺, Fe³⁺ and Ca²⁺ (Figure 2).

3. What is phytic acid built from?

An inositol ring with a phosphate on each of its six carbons (Figure 3).

4. Which mineral does phytate lower the most?

Zinc, along with iron and calcium (Figure 3).

5. Which antinutrient is high in spinach, and what does it bind?

Oxalate; it binds calcium (Figure 4).

6. What are most kidney stones made of?

Calcium oxalate (Figure 4).

7. Why drink tea between meals rather than with them if your iron is low?

Tea tannins bind nonheme iron in the meal (Figure 5).

8. How do you make dried kidney beans safe?

Soak them, then boil them hard; warming without boiling is not enough (Figure 6).

9. What does a trypsin inhibitor do, and what inactivates it?

It plugs trypsin so less protein is digested; cooking inactivates it (Figure 6).

10. What do goitrogens affect?

How the thyroid takes up or uses iodine (Figure 7).

11. Why does sourdough bread have less phytate than ordinary yeast bread?

The long fermentation activates phytase, which breaks phytate down (Figure 8).

12. Which antinutrients are destroyed by cooking, and which survive it?

Lectins and trypsin inhibitors (proteins) are destroyed; phytate, oxalate and tannins mostly survive (Figure 8).
