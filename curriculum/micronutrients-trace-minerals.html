Module 2 · Trace Minerals

The last micronutrient lesson covers the nine trace minerals, needed in amounts under 20 mg a day: iron, copper, zinc, selenium, iodine, fluoride, manganese, chromium and molybdenum. Each is shown as an ion or in a molecule that carries it, such as heme, selenocysteine and thyroxine, in ball-and-stick form, with what it does, what happens with too little or too much, and where to find it in whole foods. The figure is on the left and the lesson script on the right; the quiz is at the end.

  1. 1.1

    Trace Minerals

    The last micronutrient lesson covers the trace minerals: nine elements you need in amounts under about twenty milligrams a day, some only in micrograms.

    Title, then the root box
  2. 1.2

    Four Groups

    They're grouped by job. Iron and copper for blood and oxygen; zinc and selenium for enzymes and defense; iodine for the thyroid; and fluoride, manganese, chromium and molybdenum for teeth, bones and enzymes.

    The second and third rows, left to right
  3. 1.3

    Too Little

    The bottom row shows what happens with too little. Iron deficiency causes anemia, and iodine deficiency causes goiter. Several others are rarely short in a varied diet. Each box links to its figure.

    The bottom row
  4. 1.4

    Small Amounts, Big Jobs

    Most trace minerals sit at the active center of enzymes or other proteins, so tiny amounts matter.

    First box
  5. 1.5

    A Narrow Window

    For several of them, especially iron, selenium and fluoride, the gap between enough and too much is small, so food is safer than high-dose supplements. And soil and the rest of the meal affect how much you get.

    The last two boxes
  1. 2.1

    Two Charges

    Iron is carried as an ion with either two or three positive charges: ferrous iron and ferric iron. It switches between them by gaining or losing one electron.

    The ions, left to right
  2. 2.2

    Useful but Risky

    That easy switching is what makes iron useful for carrying oxygen and electrons. It's also why loose iron can spark damaging reactions, so your body keeps it bound to proteins.

    Note
  3. 2.3

    Heme

    Here's heme. An iron atom sits at the center, held by four nitrogens of a large flat ring made of four smaller rings: a porphyrin ring.

    Markers 1 and 2
  4. 2.4

    Oxygen Binds Here

    In hemoglobin, heme sits in a pocket of the protein, and oxygen binds to the iron from above. The two acid side chains help hold it in place.

    Marker 3
  5. 2.5

    Heme and Nonheme

    Heme iron comes only from meat, poultry and fish, and it's absorbed well. Nonheme iron, the form in plants, eggs, dairy and fortified foods, is absorbed less well and depends on the rest of the meal.

    In your body
  1. 3.1

    Absorption

    Iron is absorbed in the upper small intestine. Heme iron is taken up whole. Nonheme iron must first be changed from ferric to ferrous iron, and vitamin C helps with that.

    The first step
  2. 3.2

    Transport and Use

    In blood, iron travels bound to a protein called transferrin. About seventy percent of your iron is in hemoglobin in red blood cells and myoglobin in muscle.

    Steps two and three
  3. 3.3

    Storage and Recycling

    Spare iron is stored as ferritin in the liver, spleen and bone marrow. Old red cells are broken down and their iron reused. Your body has no active way to get rid of extra iron, so it controls how much it absorbs.

    The last two steps
  4. 3.4

    Iron Facts

    Men and women over fifty need eight milligrams a day; women nineteen to fifty need eighteen, to replace menstrual losses, and twenty-seven in pregnancy. Iron deficiency is the most common nutrient deficiency in the world, causing tiredness and anemia.

    The facts
  5. 3.5

    Too Much

    The upper limit is forty-five milligrams a day. Iron supplements are dangerous for young children, so keep them out of reach. Good sources are red meat, shellfish, sardines, lentils, beans, tofu and pumpkin seeds.

    In your body
  1. 4.1

    Zinc

    Zinc gives up two electrons and never changes charge. It holds proteins in shape, including zinc fingers that grip DNA, and it's the working part of about a hundred enzymes.

    The ion
  2. 4.2

    Zinc Facts

    It's needed for immunity, wound healing, growth, and taste and smell. Men need eleven milligrams a day, women eight. Oysters are by far the richest source, then beef, crab, pumpkin seeds and beans.

    In your body
  3. 4.3

    Copper

    Copper, like iron, switches between two charges, so it works in enzymes that move electrons. It's needed to make energy and connective tissue, and to move iron out of storage, so a shortage can cause anemia.

    The ion
  4. 4.4

    Copper Facts

    Adults need nine hundred micrograms a day, from oysters, liver, nuts, seeds, mushrooms and dark chocolate. Deficiency is rare.

    In your body
  5. 4.5

    They Compete

    Zinc and copper compete for absorption. Long-term high-dose zinc supplements can cause copper deficiency.

    Note
  1. 5.1

    Selenocysteine

    Selenium sits just below sulfur in the periodic table. Swap the sulfur in cysteine for selenium and you get selenocysteine, sometimes called the twenty-first amino acid. It's built into about twenty-five human proteins.

    Markers 1 and 2
  2. 5.2

    In Plants

    Plants take up selenium from soil and build it into selenomethionine, in place of methionine. So the selenium in plant foods depends on where they grew.

    Marker 3
  3. 5.3

    What It Does

    Selenium proteins include antioxidant enzymes and the enzymes that activate thyroid hormone. Adults need fifty-five micrograms a day. Deficiency is rare in the US.

    In your body
  4. 5.4

    Brazil Nuts

    Too much causes selenosis: garlic breath, brittle hair and nails. A single Brazil nut can contain more than a day's needs, so eat them only occasionally. Fish, eggs, poultry and whole grains are steady sources.

    Note
  1. 6.1

    Iodide

    Iodine is taken in as iodide, a large negative ion. Your thyroid gland traps it from the blood.

    The ion
  2. 6.2

    Thyroxine

    The thyroid builds iodide into thyroxine: two rings joined by an oxygen, with two iodines on each ring. Four iodines, so it's called T4. It's built from the amino acid tyrosine.

    Markers 1 to 3
  3. 6.3

    T3

    In body tissues a selenium enzyme removes one outer iodine, giving T3, the active hormone. Thyroid hormones set your metabolic rate and are essential for brain development before birth and in infancy.

    Marker 4
  4. 6.4

    Iodine Facts

    Adults need a hundred and fifty micrograms a day, two hundred and twenty in pregnancy. Too little causes goiter, an enlarged thyroid, and in pregnancy can impair the baby's brain development. Seaweed, fish, dairy, eggs and iodized salt are the main sources.

    In your body
  5. 6.5

    Not All Salt

    Sea salt, kosher salt and most salt in processed foods usually aren't iodized.

    Note
  1. 7.1

    Four Ions

    Here are the last four, as the ions or compound they're carried in. Molybdenum arrives mostly as molybdate, a molybdenum atom with four oxygens.

    Left to right
  2. 7.2

    Fluoride

    Fluoride replaces the OH in tooth enamel, making it more resistant to acid. Too little means more tooth decay; too much while teeth are forming causes white flecks. Fluoridated water and tea are the main sources.

    First row
  3. 7.3

    Manganese and Chromium

    Manganese is part of enzymes for bone and metabolism, and an antioxidant enzyme. Chromium may help insulin work, though supplements show little clear benefit. Shortage of either is rare.

    Rows two and three
  4. 7.4

    Molybdenum

    Molybdenum is a cofactor for four enzymes. Beans, lentils and whole grains supply plenty, and deficiency in healthy people is essentially unknown.

    Last row
  1. 8.1

    Helpers and Blockers

    What else is in the meal matters. Vitamin C and meat increase nonheme iron absorption. Phytate in whole grains and beans lowers iron and zinc absorption, and tea and coffee lower iron absorption.

    The table, row by row
  2. 8.2

    Easier to Absorb

    Soaking beans, sprouting grains and fermenting bread break down phytate. And cooking acidic foods in a cast-iron pan adds some iron.

    The two boxes
  3. 8.3

    Daily Values

    The Daily Values are listed in this table: eighteen milligrams of iron, eleven of zinc, a hundred and fifty micrograms of iodine, and so on.

    The table
  4. 8.4

    On the Label

    Iron must appear on every Nutrition Facts label. The others appear only if added or claimed.

    The box

    Worth Knowing

    A few extras. Heme, vitamin B12 and chlorophyll all hold a metal in the same kind of ring. Each blood donation removes about two hundred to two hundred and fifty milligrams of iron. And iodized salt, introduced in the US in 1924, all but eliminated goiter. Try the quiz below.

Quiz

Tap a question to see the answer

1. What makes a mineral a trace mineral?

You need it in amounts under about 20 mg a day (Figure 1).

2. What are the two charges iron switches between?

Fe²⁺ (ferrous) and Fe³⁺ (ferric) (Figure 2).

3. What holds the iron in heme?

Four nitrogens of a porphyrin ring (Figure 2).

4. Which foods supply heme iron?

Animal foods: meat, poultry and fish (Figure 2).

5. How does the body get rid of extra iron?

It has no active way; it controls how much it absorbs and recycles iron from old red cells (Figure 3).

6. Why do women 19–50 need more iron than men?

To replace menstrual losses (Figure 3).

7. What can long-term high-dose zinc supplements cause?

Copper deficiency (Figure 4).

8. Which food is by far the richest in zinc?

Oysters (Figure 4).

9. What is selenocysteine?

Cysteine with selenium in place of sulfur, the 21st amino acid (Figure 5).

10. Why eat Brazil nuts only occasionally?

One nut can exceed a day’s selenium needs; too much causes selenosis (Figure 5).

11. How many iodine atoms are in thyroxine (T4)? In T3?

Four; three (Figure 6).

12. Name one thing that helps and one that hinders nonheme iron absorption.

Helps: vitamin C or meat in the meal. Hinders: phytate, tea or coffee polyphenols, calcium (Figure 8).
