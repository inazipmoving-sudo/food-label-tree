Module 3 · Bioactives

Bioactives are compounds in food that are not essential nutrients but still act on the body: polyphenols such as flavonoids, carotenoids, phytosterols, the sulfur compounds of broccoli and garlic, the pungent compounds of chili, pepper and ginger, and the stimulants caffeine and theobromine. Each is shown in ball-and-stick form with what it does, the cautions, and where it is found. The figure is on the left and the lesson script on the right; the quiz is at the end.

  1. 1.1

    Bioactives

    This lesson is about bioactives: compounds in food that aren't essential nutrients, so there's no deficiency disease, but that still act on the body. Most come from plants, which make them as pigments, defenses or signals.

    Title, then the root box
  2. 1.2

    Polyphenols and Terpenoids

    The tree sorts them into four chemical families. Polyphenols, built on rings with OH groups, include flavonoids and other phenols. Terpenoids, built from five-carbon isoprene units, include carotenoids and phytosterols.

    The two left branches
  3. 1.3

    Sulfur, Spice and Stimulants

    Sulfur compounds like sulforaphane and allicin give the cabbage and onion families their bite. And the spice and stimulant compounds, capsaicin and caffeine, act on nerve receptors.

    The two right branches
  4. 1.4

    Foods

    The bottom row shows the foods richest in each. Each box links to its figure.

    The bottom row
  5. 1.5

    Whole Foods First

    Diets rich in fruit, vegetables, tea, coffee, whole grains and legumes are consistently linked to better health. But isolated bioactives in pills mostly haven't matched those results, and a few have backfired.

    The three boxes
  1. 2.1

    Phenol

    Start with phenol: an OH group on a benzene ring. A polyphenol has several. That OH easily gives up its hydrogen to quench a reactive molecule, which makes polyphenols antioxidants in a test tube.

    Marker 1
  2. 2.2

    Quercetin

    Flavonoids are the largest polyphenol group, and they all share one fifteen-carbon skeleton: two rings joined through a middle ring with an oxygen. Quercetin is the most common flavonol, in onions, kale, apples, berries and tea.

    Markers 2 to 4
  3. 2.3

    What Flavonoids Do

    Little is absorbed intact; the liver and gut bacteria change most of it, and the products may calm inflammation and help blood vessels relax. Diets high in flavonoid-rich foods are linked to lower heart disease and stroke risk.

    In your body
  4. 2.4

    Anthocyanins

    In anthocyanins, like cyanidin, the middle oxygen carries a positive charge and the molecule becomes a pigment: the red, purple and blue of berries and red cabbage. The color shifts with acidity, so red cabbage turns blue with baking soda.

    Markers 5 and 6
  5. 2.5

    Isoflavones

    In isoflavones like genistein, the second ring sits one carbon over, so it weakly fits estrogen receptors. They come mainly from soy, and normal intake of soy foods is considered safe.

    Markers 7 and 8
  1. 3.1

    Resveratrol

    Resveratrol is two phenol rings joined by a double bond, made by grapes to fight fungus. It extended lifespan in some animals, but human trials have disappointed, and a glass of red wine has only a tiny amount.

    Marker 1
  2. 3.2

    Chlorogenic Acid

    Chlorogenic acid is quinic acid joined through an ester to caffeic acid, the phenol part. It's the main polyphenol in coffee, adding bitterness and acidity.

    Markers 2 to 4
  3. 3.3

    Coffee

    For many adults coffee is the biggest source of polyphenols, and moderate coffee drinking is linked to lower risk of type 2 diabetes and liver disease. Roasting breaks chlorogenic acid down, so light roasts keep more.

    In your body
  4. 3.4

    Hydroxytyrosol

    Hydroxytyrosol has the same pair of neighboring OH groups as quercetin and caffeic acid. It's the main polyphenol of extra-virgin olive oil and part of its peppery bite.

    Marker 5
  5. 3.5

    Olive Oil

    The EU allows a claim that olive oil polyphenols help protect blood lipids from oxidation, for oils with enough of them. Refining removes most, which is one reason extra-virgin oil is thought to do more.

    In your body
  1. 4.1

    Isoprene

    Terpenoids are built from isoprene, a five-carbon unit with one branch. Eight of them make a carotenoid.

    Marker 1
  2. 4.2

    Beta-Carotene

    Beta-carotene has a ring at each end joined by a long chain of alternating double bonds. That chain absorbs blue light, so it looks orange. Cut in the middle, it makes two molecules of vitamin A.

    Markers 2 to 4
  3. 4.3

    Too Much

    Your gut only converts what you need, so food beta-carotene can't cause vitamin A poisoning; a lot just tints the skin orange, harmlessly. But high-dose supplements raised lung cancer risk in smokers, so smokers should avoid them.

  4. 4.4

    Lycopene

    Lycopene has the same formula but open ends, and its fully flat chain makes it red. It's the color of tomatoes and watermelon and isn't turned into vitamin A. Cooking with a little fat makes it far more available, so tomato sauce beats raw tomatoes.

    Markers 5 and 6
  5. 4.5

    Lutein

    Two more: lutein and zeaxanthin collect in the eye's macula and filter blue light. Kale, spinach, corn and egg yolk are good sources.

  1. 5.1

    Two Sterols

    Here's cholesterol from the Lipids lesson: four fused rings, an OH and a carbon tail. Next to it is beta-sitosterol, the main plant sterol, which is the same molecule with two extra carbons on the tail.

    Markers 1 and 2
  2. 5.2

    Crowding Out

    Because they look so alike, plant sterols crowd cholesterol out of the bile mixtures that carry it into gut cells. Less cholesterol is absorbed, both from food and from what your liver sends into the gut.

    In your body
  3. 5.3

    How Much

    About two grams a day lowers LDL cholesterol by roughly eight to ten percent within a few weeks, and the FDA and EU allow heart-health claims for foods with added sterols or stanols.

    In your body
  4. 5.4

    Found In

    They're in vegetable oils, nuts, seeds, wheat germ and legumes, but a normal diet gives only a fraction of a gram, so reaching two grams takes fortified spreads, yogurts or drinks.

  5. 5.5

    Who Should Avoid

    Very little is absorbed. People with the rare disorder sitosterolemia do absorb them and should avoid them.

  1. 6.1

    Glucoraphanin

    Broccoli stores glucoraphanin, a glucosinolate: a sulfur chain parked on a glucose. When you cut or chew it, the enzyme myrosinase cuts the glucose off, and the rest rearranges into sulforaphane, with an N=C=S group.

    Markers 1 to 3
  2. 6.2

    What It Does

    Sulforaphane switches on Nrf2, a signal that raises the body's own detoxifying and antioxidant enzymes. Brassica-rich diets are linked to lower risk of some cancers; trials of sulforaphane itself are early.

    In your body
  3. 6.3

    Getting the Most

    To get the most, chop or chew it raw or lightly steamed. Hard boiling destroys myrosinase, but a pinch of mustard powder on cooked broccoli adds it back. Broccoli sprouts have by far the most.

    The steps, left to right
  4. 6.4

    Allicin

    Garlic works the same way. Crushing it lets the enzyme alliinase make allicin, two sulfurs side by side. It soon breaks down into the sulfur compounds that carry garlic's smell.

    Marker 4
  5. 6.5

    Garlic Evidence

    Garlic's sulfur compounds kill microbes in the lab and may slightly lower blood pressure and cholesterol, though the evidence is modest. Onions, leeks and chives make related compounds, including the one that makes you cry.

    In your body
  1. 7.1

    Capsaicin

    Capsaicin has a vanilla-like ring head, an amide link and a fatty tail. That tail is why it dissolves in fat and milk, not water.

    Markers 1 to 3
  2. 7.2

    Why It Burns

    Capsaicin switches on TRPV1, the nerve receptor for dangerous heat, so your mouth feels burning with no real burn. With repeated exposure the nerves become less sensitive, which is why capsaicin creams are used for pain.

    In your body
  3. 7.3

    Cooling Down

    Very hot peppers can cause pain and vomiting. Milk and fatty foods cool the burn better than water. Most of the heat is in the white ribs, not the seeds.

  4. 7.4

    Piperine

    Black pepper's piperine also has an amide and also triggers heat receptors, more mildly. It slows the breakdown of some compounds, which is why it's paired with turmeric.

    Marker 4
  5. 7.5

    Gingerol

    Ginger's gingerol has capsaicin's head with a ketone and OH in its chain; drying or cooking turns it into the even sharper shogaol. Ginger modestly eases nausea in several trials.

    Marker 5
  1. 8.1

    Caffeine

    Caffeine is a purine, related to the A and G of DNA, with three methyl groups. Theobromine, from cocoa, has an N-H in place of one.

    Markers 1 and 2
  2. 8.2

    How It Works

    Caffeine blocks adenosine, the brain signal that builds up sleepiness. It takes effect in fifteen to forty-five minutes and lasts, with a half-life of about five hours.

    In your body
  3. 8.3

    How Much Is Safe

    The FDA says up to 400 milligrams a day generally isn't linked to harm in healthy adults; in pregnancy the guidance is 200 or less. Too much brings jitters, a racing heart and poor sleep. And theobromine is toxic to dogs.

  4. 8.4

    Caffeine Amounts

    A cup of brewed coffee has about 80 to 100 milligrams, black tea about half that, cola about 30 to 40, and an ounce of dark chocolate 12 to 25.

  2. 9.2

    Worth Knowing

    A few extras. Test-tube antioxidant scores don't predict effects in the body. Grapefruit contains bioactives that raise the blood levels of many medicines. And some compounds, like tannins and glucosinolates, are antinutrients or bioactives depending on the dose and the diet.

Quiz

Tap a question to see the answer

1. What makes a compound a bioactive rather than a nutrient?

It acts on the body but is not essential, so there is no deficiency disease (Figure 1).

2. What is a phenol, and what makes a polyphenol?

An OH group on a benzene ring; a polyphenol has several (Figure 2).

3. Why does red cabbage turn blue when cooked with baking soda?

Its anthocyanins change color with acidity: red in acid, blue-green in alkali (Figure 2).

4. Which food is the main source of isoflavones, and how do they act?

Soy; they act like a weak estrogen (Figure 2).

5. Which polyphenol is the main one in coffee?

Chlorogenic acid (Figure 3).

6. What five-carbon unit are carotenoids and sterols built from?

Isoprene (Figure 4).

7. How do beta-carotene and lycopene differ?

Beta-carotene has a ring at each end and is provitamin A; lycopene is an open chain, red, and not made into vitamin A (Figure 4).

8. How do phytosterols lower LDL cholesterol?

They crowd cholesterol out of the bile mixtures that carry it into gut cells, so less is absorbed (Figure 5).

9. What enzyme makes sulforaphane, and when does it act?

Myrosinase, when broccoli is cut or chewed; hard boiling destroys it (Figure 6).

10. Which receptor does capsaicin switch on, and what cools the burn best?

TRPV1, the heat receptor; milk or other fatty foods (Figure 7).

11. How does caffeine make you feel alert?

It blocks adenosine, the brain signal for sleepiness (Figure 8).

12. What daily caffeine amount does the FDA say is generally safe for healthy adults?

Up to 400 mg, about four cups of brewed coffee (Figure 8).
