Module 2 · Fat-Soluble Vitamins

Module 2 begins with the micronutrients: substances you need in milligrams or micrograms rather than grams. This lesson covers the four fat-soluble vitamins, A, D, E and K. All four are lipids built from five-carbon isoprene units, which is why they are absorbed with fat and stored in the body. Each is shown as a ball-and-stick model, with what it does, what happens with too little or too much, and where to find it in whole foods. The figure is on the left and the lesson script on the right; the quiz is at the end.

  1. 1.1

    Micronutrients

    Module 2 is about micronutrients: vitamins and minerals you need in milligrams or micrograms, not grams. They give no calories, but nearly every process in your body depends on them. We start with the four fat-soluble vitamins: A, D, E and K.

    Title, then the root box
  2. 1.2

    Four Vitamins

    Each vitamin across the top links to its own figure. Vitamin A is for vision and growth, D for calcium and bone, E is an antioxidant, and K is needed for blood clotting.

    The four vitamin boxes
  3. 1.3

    Their Forms

    Each vitamin comes in more than one form. Vitamin A comes ready-made as retinol, or as beta-carotene, which your body converts. D3 comes from skin and animal foods, D2 from fungi. Vitamin E is a family; alpha-tocopherol is the one you need. And K1 comes from plants, K2 from bacteria.

    The two rows below, column by column
  4. 1.4

    What They Share

    All four are lipids built from the same five-carbon units, so they dissolve in fat rather than water. They need fat and bile to be absorbed, and they're stored in your liver and fat tissue, so you don't need them every day.

    First two boxes
  5. 1.5

    Storage Cuts Both Ways

    Because they build up, large supplement doses of vitamins A and D can be harmful. Food sources almost never cause too much.

    Third box
  1. 2.1

    Isoprene

    Here's isoprene: five carbons, a four-carbon chain with a one-carbon branch, and two double bonds. Plants and animals join these units head to tail to build long, oily molecules, including cholesterol, carotenoids and all four fat-soluble vitamins.

    Isoprene, marker 1
  2. 2.2

    Retinol

    This is retinol, vitamin A: four isoprene units, twenty carbons. The ring on the left comes from the first unit folded around. The chain has alternating single and double bonds.

    Markers 1 and 2
  3. 2.3

    One OH Is Not Enough

    Retinol has just one OH group, at the right-hand end. That small water-loving patch is outweighed by thirty hydrogens of oily chain. Compare glucose, with an OH on almost every carbon: glucose dissolves in water, retinol dissolves in fat.

    Marker 3
  4. 2.4

    Fat- Versus Water-Soluble

    This table sums up the difference. Fat-soluble vitamins are absorbed with fat into the lymph and stored for weeks to months. Water-soluble vitamins go straight into the blood, and most of any extra leaves in urine.

    The table, row by row
  5. 2.5

    Why It Matters

    The practical upshot: you can build up stores of fat-soluble vitamins, which protects you through short gaps, but also means megadoses can accumulate to harmful levels.

    Note
  1. 3.1

    Retinol to Retinal

    Retinol ends in CH2OH. Take off two hydrogens and it becomes retinal, ending in CH double bond O. Retinal can go on to become retinoic acid, which switches genes on and off for cell growth.

    Retinol, marker 1, then retinal, marker 2
  2. 3.2

    How You See

    Retinal is drawn straight here. In your eye it sits bent at one double bond, inside a protein. When light hits it, it straightens, and that change in shape is the very first step of seeing.

    Note
  3. 3.3

    What Vitamin A Does

    Vitamin A is needed for vision, especially in dim light, for healthy skin and the linings of the eyes, gut and lungs, for immune defense, and for growth. Too little causes night blindness and then damage to the eyes; worldwide, it's a leading cause of preventable blindness in children. Too much preformed vitamin A, mostly from supplements, builds up in the liver and can damage it, and in pregnancy can cause birth defects. You find it in liver, fish liver oils, oily fish, eggs and dairy.

    In your body
  4. 3.4

    Beta-Carotene

    Beta-carotene is the orange pigment in carrots: eight isoprene units, with the same ring at each end. A gut enzyme cuts it in the middle, and each half becomes retinal. That's why it's called provitamin A.

    Markers 1 and 2
  5. 3.5

    Safe from Food

    Your body only converts beta-carotene as needed, so food sources don't cause vitamin A poisoning. Eating a lot can turn your skin slightly orange, which is harmless. High-dose supplements, though, raised lung cancer risk in smokers in two large trials. Find it in carrots, sweet potato, squash, leafy greens, mango and apricots.

    In your body
  1. 4.1

    Made in Your Skin

    Vitamin D is unusual: your skin can make it. It starts with 7-dehydrocholesterol, which is cholesterol with one extra double bond in the second ring.

    The top molecule, marker 1
  2. 4.2

    Sunlight Opens a Ring

    When UVB light hits the skin, it breaks the second ring open at this bond. With a little body heat, the result is vitamin D3. Count the atoms: same formula, nothing added. Vitamin D is a steroid with a broken ring.

    Markers 2, 3 and 4
  3. 4.3

    D2

    Vitamin D2 is almost identical: one extra double bond and one extra carbon in the tail. Fungi and yeast make it from their own sterol when exposed to UV light.

    Markers 1 and 2
  4. 4.4

    Activation

    Neither form does anything yet. The liver adds an OH to make 25-hydroxyvitamin D, the form a blood test measures. Then the kidney adds a second OH, making calcitriol, the active hormone. Calcitriol raises calcium and phosphate absorption from the gut and keeps bones mineralized.

    The four steps
  5. 4.5

    Needs and Sources

    The RDA is fifteen micrograms, six hundred units, a day for ages one to seventy, and twenty micrograms over seventy. Too little causes soft bones: rickets in children, osteomalacia in adults. Too much, only from supplements, raises blood calcium. Few foods have much: oily fish, cod liver oil, egg yolks, and UV-exposed mushrooms. That's why milk is often fortified.

    The three boxes, then the food line
  1. 5.1

    Alpha-Tocopherol

    Here's alpha-tocopherol, the main form of vitamin E. It has a two-ring head carrying an OH group and three methyl groups, and a long sixteen-carbon tail.

    Alpha-tocopherol, markers 1 and 3
  2. 5.2

    Head and Tail

    The tail anchors the molecule inside fatty cell membranes. The OH on the head is the working part: it can give up its hydrogen to stop a chain reaction of damage.

    Marker 1, then marker 3
  3. 5.3

    Alpha Versus Gamma

    Gamma-tocopherol is the same except for one methyl group on the ring, replaced by a plain hydrogen. It's actually the most common form in the US diet, from soybean and corn oils. But the liver keeps only the alpha form in the blood, so only alpha counts toward your needs.

    Marker 2 on both
  4. 5.4

    Stopping a Chain Reaction

    Polyunsaturated fats in membranes are easily damaged. A reactive oxygen molecule steals a hydrogen from one, which then attacks its neighbor, and so on. Vitamin E hands over a hydrogen and stops the chain. Vitamin C helps restore it afterwards.

    The four steps
  5. 5.5

    Needs and Sources

    The RDA is fifteen milligrams of alpha-tocopherol a day. Deficiency is rare, mostly in people who can't absorb fat, and causes nerve and muscle damage. Supplements above the upper limit, a thousand milligrams a day, raise bleeding risk. The best sources are sunflower seeds, almonds, hazelnuts, wheat germ, and sunflower and safflower oils.

    In your body
  1. 6.1

    The Working Head

    Every form of vitamin K has the same head: a double ring with two C double bond O groups. That head does the chemistry.

    K1, marker 1
  2. 6.2

    K1 and K2

    What differs is the tail. K1, phylloquinone, made by plants in their leaves, has a mostly saturated tail. K2, the menaquinones, have tails that keep a double bond in every isoprene unit. MK-4 has four units; bacteria make longer ones, like MK-7 in natto.

    Marker 2 on K1, then K2, marker 3
  3. 6.3

    Clotting

    The liver makes several clotting proteins in an inactive form. Vitamin K helps add an extra acid group to some of their amino acids, which lets them hold calcium and gather at a wound to form a clot. The same change activates calcium-binding proteins in bone.

    The four steps
  4. 6.4

    Newborns

    Very little vitamin K crosses the placenta and breast milk is low in it, so newborns get a vitamin K injection at birth to prevent bleeding. In adults, deficiency is rare.

    In your body
  5. 6.5

    Needs and Sources

    The adequate intake is a hundred and twenty micrograms a day for men and ninety for women. No harm is known from high intakes. But people taking the blood thinner warfarin are advised to keep their vitamin K intake steady, because it counteracts the drug. K1 is in kale, spinach, collards, broccoli and soybean and canola oils; K2 in natto, cheese and egg yolks.

    In your body
  1. 7.1

    With Fat

    Fat-soluble vitamins are absorbed far better with a little fat in the same meal. Bile mixes them into tiny droplets, the gut wall packs them into chylomicrons with the fat, and they travel through the lymph before reaching the blood and liver.

    The first four steps
  2. 7.2

    Where They Are Stored

    Vitamin A is stored mainly in the liver; D and E in fat tissue and the liver. Vitamin K is used up fastest.

    The last step
  3. 7.3

    Forms on the Label

    On ingredient lists you'll see vitamin A as retinyl palmitate or acetate, or as beta-carotene, which is also used as a color. D3 is cholecalciferol and D2 ergocalciferol. Natural vitamin E is d-alpha-tocopherol; the synthetic dl- form has about half the activity. Tocopherols are also added to oils as antioxidants, as E306 to E309.

    The table, row by row
  4. 7.4

    Daily Values

    The Daily Values are nine hundred micrograms of vitamin A, twenty micrograms of D, fifteen milligrams of E, and a hundred and twenty micrograms of K. Since the 2016 update, US labels must list vitamin D; A, E and K appear only if added or claimed.

  2. 8.2

    Worth Knowing

    A few extras. Eat colorful vegetables with a little fat to absorb their carotenoids and vitamin K. Liver is so rich in vitamin A that it's best eaten occasionally, especially in pregnancy. Sunlight can't give you too much vitamin D, but supplements can. And these vitamins are close relatives of cholesterol, built from the same isoprene units. Try the quiz below.

Quiz

Tap a question to see the answer

1. Why do vitamins A, D, E and K dissolve in fat rather than water?

They are made mostly of carbon and hydrogen (built from isoprene units), with few OH groups (Figure 2).

2. What is isoprene?

A five-carbon building block, C5H8, that plants and animals join to make molecules like retinol and carotenoids (Figure 2).

3. What is the difference between retinol and retinal?

Retinol ends in CH2OH; retinal ends in CH=O (two hydrogens fewer) (Figure 3).

4. Why is beta-carotene called provitamin A?

The body can cut it in the middle and turn each half into retinal (Figure 3).

5. Why is preformed vitamin A a concern in pregnancy?

Large amounts, mainly from supplements or lots of liver, can cause birth defects (Figure 3).

6. What does UVB light do to 7-dehydrocholesterol in the skin?

It breaks open the second ring, forming vitamin D3 (Figure 4).

7. Where is vitamin D activated?

An OH is added in the liver, then a second in the kidney, making calcitriol (Figure 4).

8. Name two deficiency diseases of vitamin D.

Rickets in children, osteomalacia in adults (Figure 4).

9. What part of vitamin E does the antioxidant work?

The OH on its ring head, which gives up a hydrogen to stop a chain reaction (Figure 5).

10. How do alpha- and gamma-tocopherol differ?

Gamma lacks one methyl group on the ring; only alpha counts toward vitamin E needs (Figure 5).

11. Why do newborns get a vitamin K injection?

Little vitamin K crosses the placenta and breast milk is low in it, so babies risk bleeding (Figure 6).

12. Which vitamin must be listed on every US Nutrition Facts label?

Vitamin D (Figure 7).
